Chapter 2 — Dominance and Iterated Elimination

Predict behaviour using rationality alone, before any equilibrium concept.
What you will be able to do
  • Distinguish strict from weak dominance and check the 'for every opponent action' clause.
  • Run iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies to solve a 3x3 game.
  • Explain why each round of elimination consumes one more level of common knowledge of rationality.
  • State why strict elimination is order-independent but weak elimination is not.
  • Detect a strategy dominated by a mixture rather than by any pure strategy.

1. When one strategy is simply worse

Before asking what a player will do, ask what a player will never do. Sometimes a strategy is worse than another no matter what the opponents do. Such a strategy can be deleted with a clear conscience, and deleting it often collapses a big game to a small one.

Definitions - dominance

\(s_i'\) is strictly dominated by \(s_i\) if

\[ u_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \;\gt \; u_i(s_i',\, s_{-i}) \qquad \text{for every } s_{-i}. \]

\(s_i'\) is weakly dominated by \(s_i\) if the inequality holds with \(\ge\) for every \(s_{-i}\) and with \(\gt \) for at least one \(s_{-i}\).

The word every is the whole content of the definition. A strategy that is worse in three columns out of four is not dominated; the fourth column may be exactly the situation you find yourself in.

2. Solving the Prisoner's Dilemma by dominance

For player 1: against Cooperate, Defect gives 5 versus 3; against Defect, Defect gives 1 versus 0. Defect wins in every column, so Cooperate is strictly dominated. By symmetry the same holds for player 2. A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy, so the outcome is (Defect, Defect) with payoffs (1, 1). Note how little we needed: no beliefs about the opponent, no equilibrium concept, no fixed point. Dominance arguments are the cheapest predictions in game theory, which is why they are the most persuasive when they are available.

3. Iterated elimination

Deleting a dominated strategy shrinks the game. In the smaller game, another strategy may now be dominated even though it was not before - because the column that protected it has disappeared. Repeat until nothing more can be deleted. This is iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS).

Step 1. Compare player 2's columns. Against Top, 2 gets 3 / 1 / 2; against Middle, 1 / 4 / 6; against Bottom, 0 / 6 / 8. Is Centre dominated by Right? Compare 1 vs 2, 4 vs 6, 6 vs 8: Right is strictly better in all three rows, so Centre is strictly dominated and is deleted.

Step 2. In the remaining 3x2 game (columns Left and Right), look at player 1: Top gives (4, 6), Middle gives (2, 3), Bottom gives (3, 2). Top strictly beats Middle (4 > 2 and 6 > 3) and strictly beats Bottom (4 > 3 and 6 > 2). Delete Middle and Bottom.

Step 3. Only row Top remains. Player 2 now compares 3 (Left) with 2 (Right) and keeps Left. The unique surviving profile is (Top, Left) with payoffs (4, 3). A game solvable this way is called dominance solvable.

Why iteration needs common knowledge

Each round of elimination uses one more layer of reasoning about reasoning. Round 1 needs 'player 2 is rational'. Round 2 needs 'player 1 knows player 2 is rational'. Round 3 needs 'player 2 knows that player 1 knows that player 2 is rational'. IESDS therefore consumes common knowledge of rationality one level at a time.

4. Strict versus weak: an important asymmetry

The order in which you delete strictly dominated strategies does not matter - the final set is the same whichever path you take. This is a genuine theorem, and it makes IESDS a well-defined procedure.

Careful

Weak dominance is order-dependent. Deleting weakly dominated strategies in different orders can leave different survivors, and can destroy legitimate equilibria. Use weak elimination with care and report the order you used.

5. Dominance by a mixed strategy

A strategy can fail to be dominated by any single strategy yet be dominated by a randomisation over two others. Consider player 1 facing two columns:

Strategy C gives 2 in both columns. Neither A (6 then 0) nor B (0 then 6) dominates it. But the mixed strategy \(\sigma = \tfrac{1}{2} A + \tfrac{1}{2} B\) yields an expected \(\tfrac{1}{2}(6) + \tfrac{1}{2}(0) = 3\) in both columns, and \(3 \gt 2\). So C is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy and a rational expected-payoff maximiser never plays it. When you check for dominance, you must therefore consider mixtures, not only pure strategies. Chapter 4 develops mixed strategies properly.

6. Rationalizability, in one paragraph

A strategy is rationalizable if it is a best response to some belief about the opponents that is itself consistent with their rationality, and so on up the hierarchy. In two-player games the set of rationalizable strategies coincides exactly with the set surviving iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (Bernheim 1984, Pearce 1984). Rationalizability is thus the largest prediction you can make from common knowledge of rationality alone. Nash equilibrium, in Chapter 3, is a strictly stronger requirement: it additionally demands that beliefs be correct.

Check your understanding

  1. A strategy is worse in 4 of a rival's 5 columns and better in the 5th. Is it dominated? (Answer: No - dominance requires every column)
  2. Why is it safe to delete strictly dominated strategies in any order? (Answer: The surviving set is provably the same for every order)
  3. Which mixture over A and B in section 5 dominates C? (Answer: \(\tfrac{1}{2}A + \tfrac{1}{2}B\), giving expected \(3 \gt 2\) in both columns)
  4. What extra assumption does IESDS need beyond each player being rational? (Answer: Common knowledge of rationality, one extra level per round)
