Chapter 1 — What Is a Game? Players, Strategies and Payoffs

Build the vocabulary and the payoff matrix that the whole course runs on.
What you will be able to do
  • Say precisely what a game is: players, strategy sets, payoff functions.
  • Read and write a two-player payoff matrix without confusing whose number is whose.
  • Compute a best response to a fixed profile of opponents' strategies.
  • Recognise the Prisoner's Dilemma, Stag Hunt, Chicken and Matching Pennies on sight.
  • State the rationality and common-knowledge assumptions, and say what they do not claim.

1. Why we need a special theory

Most of what you learned in earlier courses was decision theory: one person faces a world that does not care what they do. A farmer choosing when to harvest faces weather; the weather has no opinion about the farmer. Game theory begins the moment the thing you are reacting to is reacting back. When you undercut a competitor's price, the competitor answers. When you bluff at poker, your opponent is trying to read you reading them.

This circularity - my best action depends on your action, which depends on mine - is what makes strategic situations hard, and it is exactly what game theory was invented to tame. The founding text is von Neumann and Morgenstern's Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944).

2. The three ingredients of a game

Stripped to essentials, a game is three lists.

Two remarks that trip up beginners. First, payoffs are utilities, not necessarily money: they already include everything the player cares about - spite, fairness, reputation, prison time. If a player would rather have less money and a clean conscience, that is a fact about their payoff function, not a violation of rationality. Second, a strategy is a complete plan. In a one-shot simultaneous game a strategy is just an action, but later, in games with several stages, a strategy will have to say what to do at every point where the player might be called on to move.

3. The payoff matrix

When there are two players with finitely many strategies each, the whole game fits in a table. Rows are the strategies of player 1 (the row player), columns those of player 2 (the column player). Each cell holds a pair: the row player's payoff first, the column player's second. The order is a convention, and it is worth saying out loud every time you read a matrix, because reversing it silently reverses your conclusions.

Here is the most famous game in the subject, the Prisoner's Dilemma. Two arrested accomplices are questioned separately. Each may stay silent (Cooperate with the other prisoner) or testify (Defect). Payoffs are years of freedom gained, so bigger is better.

Read the cell (Defect, Cooperate) = (5, 0): player 1 defected, player 2 cooperated, so player 1 gets 5 and player 2 gets 0. Notice already the paradox that gave the game its name. Whatever player 2 does, player 1 earns more by defecting (5 > 3 if 2 cooperates; 1 > 0 if 2 defects). The same is true for player 2. So both defect and both get 1 - even though both would have preferred the 3, 3 they could have had. Individual rationality and collective rationality come apart. We will make this argument precise in Chapter 2.

4. Best responses

The central computational move in all of game theory is: fix what everybody else does, then ask what is best for me. Write \(s_{-i}\) for the strategies of everyone except \(i\). Player \(i\)'s strategy \(s_i^{*}\) is a best response to \(s_{-i}\) if

Definition - best response
\[ u_i(s_i^{*},\, s_{-i}) \;\ge\; u_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \qquad \text{for every } s_i \in S_i. \]

A practical trick for 2-player matrices: go column by column and underline the row player's best payoff in that column; then go row by row and underline the column player's best payoff in that row. Cells where both numbers are underlined are the cells we will call Nash equilibria in Chapter 3. Try it on the matrix above - only (Defect, Defect) survives.

5. A small gallery of games

Four two-by-two games appear constantly. Learning to recognise them is like learning to recognise chord shapes.

Matching Pennies has no cell where both players are simultaneously happy - whichever cell you point at, one player wants to move. That is not a defect in the game; it is a signal that the players should randomise. Chapter 4 develops that idea.

6. What we are assuming

Standard game theory rests on assumptions worth stating plainly, because the interesting modern work consists largely of relaxing them.

Careful

A frequent misreading. Game theory does not claim people are selfish. It claims they act to maximise their own payoff function, and that function may assign high value to a friend's wellbeing. Selfishness is an assumption about the content of payoffs; rationality is an assumption about their use.

7. Two dimensions for classifying games

Two questions organise the rest of the course. Timing: do players move at the same time (simultaneous, or strategic form, Chapters 1-5) or one after another while observing history (sequential, or extensive form, Chapter 6)? Interests: is one player's gain exactly the other's loss (zero-sum, Chapter 5) or is there room for shared gains (non-zero-sum, everywhere else)? Independently, a game is cooperative if binding agreements are possible (Chapter 10) and non-cooperative otherwise - which is the default for everything before that.

Glossary

TermMeaning
PlayerAny entity that chooses a strategy and has payoffs over outcomes.
StrategyA complete plan of action for a player, specifying a choice at every point where the player may have to move.
Strategy profileOne strategy for each player: \(s = (s_1, \dots, s_n)\). It determines the outcome.
Payoff (utility)A number measuring how much a player values an outcome; already includes all the player's concerns.
Normal (strategic) formThe description of a game as the triple (players, strategy sets, payoff functions).
Best responseA strategy \(s_i^{*}\) maximising \(u_i(\cdot,\, s_{-i})\) for fixed strategies of the others.
Zero-sum gameA game in which payoffs in every cell sum to a constant (usually zero): pure conflict.
Common knowledgeA fact everyone knows, everyone knows everyone knows, ad infinitum.

Check your understanding

  1. In the Prisoner's Dilemma above, what does player 2 get in the cell (Cooperate, Defect)? (Answer: 5 - the second number in that cell)
  2. Is (Stag, Hare) in the Stag Hunt a pair of mutual best responses? (Answer: No: hunter 1 gets 0 and would rather play Hare for 3)
  3. Why can Matching Pennies not have a cell where neither player wants to deviate? (Answer: It is zero-sum with no matching interest; one player always wants to switch)
  4. A player prefers giving money to charity over keeping it. Does that break the rationality assumption? (Answer: No - it is a statement about their payoff function)
