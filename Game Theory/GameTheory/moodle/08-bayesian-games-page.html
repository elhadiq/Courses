Bayesian Games and Incomplete Information

Types, priors, signalling and screening: playing when you do not know who you face.

  • Apply Harsanyi's transformation: types, common prior, and strategies as functions of type.

1. Not knowing who you are playing

Everything so far assumed the payoff matrix is common knowledge. Usually it is not. A bidder does not know rivals' valuations; an insurer does not know an applicant's risk; a firm does not know a rival's costs. These are games of incomplete information.

Careful

Do not confuse incomplete with imperfect information. Imperfect information (Chapter 6) means you do not know what has been played. Incomplete information means you do not know the game - somebody's payoffs are unknown to you.

2. Harsanyi's transformation

John Harsanyi (1967-68) showed how to convert incomplete information into imperfect information, which we already know how to handle. Each player is assigned a type \(\theta_i\) that summarises everything privately known to them - typically their payoff function. Nature moves first and draws the type profile from a common prior distribution; each player observes only their own type.

Definition - Bayesian game

A Bayesian game is the tuple

\[ \bigl\langle N,\; (A_i)_{i \in N},\; (\Theta_i)_{i \in N},\; p,\; (u_i)_{i \in N} \bigr\rangle, \qquad u_i : A \times \Theta \to \mathbb{R}, \]

where \(p\) is the common prior on \(\Theta = \prod_i \Theta_i\). A strategy is now a function \(s_i : \Theta_i \to A_i\), telling each type what to do.

The strategy-as-function point is the one to hold on to. You do not choose an action; you choose a rule mapping what you learn about yourself into an action. When you evaluate that rule you must average over the types your opponents might have, using Bayes' rule to update from the prior given anything you have observed.

Definition - Bayesian Nash equilibrium

A profile \(s^{*}\) is a Bayesian Nash equilibrium if for every player \(i\) and every type \(\theta_i \in \Theta_i\),

\[ s_i^{*}(\theta_i) \;\in\; \arg\max_{a_i \in A_i} \; \sum_{\theta_{-i}} p(\theta_{-i} \mid \theta_i)\, u_i\bigl(a_i, s_{-i}^{*}(\theta_{-i}), \theta\bigr). \]

Each type best-responds in expectation over the opponents' types and their equilibrium actions.

3. A worked example: entry with an unknown incumbent

An entrant considers entering. The incumbent is tough (fighting is cheap, payoff \(-1\) rather than a loss) with probability \(\mu\), and soft with probability \(1 - \mu\). If the incumbent is tough it fights; if soft it accommodates. The entrant gets \(-2\) if fought, \(+2\) if accommodated, and \(0\) by staying out. The entrant cannot observe the type - only the prior.

\[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{\text{entrant}}(\text{In})\bigr] = \mu(-2) + (1-\mu)(2) = 2 - 4\mu, \]
\[ \text{so the entrant enters iff } \; 2 - 4\mu \gt 0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \mu \lt \tfrac{1}{2}. \]

So the entrant enters when it is sufficiently confident the incumbent is soft. Two lessons follow, and they drive an enormous literature. First, the incumbent now has a reason to build a reputation for toughness - even a soft incumbent may fight early entrants to raise \(\mu\) in later entrants' minds. Second, information itself becomes strategic: the incumbent may prefer that the entrant remain uncertain, while the entrant will pay for a signal.

4. Signalling and screening

When the informed party moves first to reveal something, we have signalling. Spence's (1973) education model is the classic: education may raise no productivity at all, yet high-ability workers acquire it because it is cheaper for them than for low-ability workers, and employers rationally read the diploma as evidence. The equilibrium concept is perfect Bayesian equilibrium: strategies optimal given beliefs, beliefs updated by Bayes' rule wherever possible.

When the uninformed party moves first, offering a menu designed so that different types self-select, we have screening. Insurance contracts trading a lower premium against a higher deductible are the standard example: low-risk customers accept the deductible, high-risk customers do not.

5. Adverse selection: the market for lemons

Akerlof's (1970) used-car market shows how private information can destroy a market outright. Sellers know quality, buyers do not. Buyers will pay only the expected quality of cars on offer; at that price, owners of above-average cars withdraw; the average quality of what remains falls; the price falls again. The market can unravel until only the worst cars - the lemons - trade, even though many mutually beneficial trades exist. Warranties, certification, brands and return policies are all institutional responses to this failure. Akerlof, Spence and Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel prize for this body of work.

6. A Bayesian game solved by types

Two firms compete in quantities with \(P = 100 - Q\). Firm 1's unit cost is 10 and is common knowledge. Firm 2's cost is \(c_2 = 10\) with probability \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(c_2 = 4\) with probability \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), known only to firm 2. Firm 2 has two types and therefore two quantities to choose; firm 1 chooses one quantity, best-responding to the average of firm 2's two types.

\[ q_2(c_2) = \frac{100 - c_2 - q_1}{2}, \qquad q_1 = \frac{90 - \mathbb{E}[q_2]}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; q_1 = \frac{87 + q_1}{4}. \]
\[ \boxed{\; q_1 = 29, \qquad q_2(10) = 30.5, \qquad q_2(4) = 33.5. \;} \]

The efficient low-cost type produces more; firm 1 hedges between the two possibilities rather than tailoring its output to either, and produces slightly less than the 30 it would choose if firm 2's cost were known to be 10.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Incomplete informationSome players do not know other players' payoff functions.
Imperfect informationSome players do not observe earlier moves; the payoff structure is still known.
Type\(\theta_i \in \Theta_i\): a player's private information, summarising everything they know that others do not.
Common priorThe shared probability distribution over type profiles from which nature draws.
Bayesian Nash equilibriumEach type of each player best-responds in expectation over opponents' types and actions.
SignallingThe informed party acts first in a way that credibly conveys their type.
ScreeningThe uninformed party offers a menu that induces types to self-select.
Adverse selectionPrivate information causes the best-quality participants to withdraw, degrading the market.
Separating equilibriumDifferent types choose different actions, so type is revealed.
Pooling equilibriumAll types choose the same action, so nothing is revealed.

Check your understanding

  1. Is not knowing a rival's cost incomplete or imperfect information? (Answer: Incomplete - the payoffs themselves are unknown)
  2. What object is a strategy in a Bayesian game? (Answer: A function from the player's own type to an action)
  3. In the entry example, at what prior probability of a tough incumbent does entry stop? (Answer: When \(\mu \ge \tfrac{1}{2}\))
  4. Why can a used-car market unravel? (Answer: Buyers pay the average, good cars withdraw, the average falls, and so on)
