Nash Equilibrium in Pure Strategies

The central solution concept: mutual best responses, and what it does and does not promise.

What you will be able to do
  • State the definition of Nash equilibrium and verify a candidate profile from the definition.
  • Find all pure Nash equilibria of a matrix game with the underline method.
  • Explain why a Nash equilibrium can be Pareto dominated, using the Prisoner's Dilemma.
  • Derive best-response functions and solve the Cournot duopoly for its equilibrium quantities.
  • Discuss multiplicity, focal points, and the correctness-of-beliefs assumption.

1. The idea: no regrets, given what the others did

Dominance solves few games. We need a concept that applies everywhere. John Nash's answer (1950) is disarmingly simple: look for a strategy profile at which no single player, acting alone, would want to change their strategy, taking everyone else's strategy as given.

Definition - Nash equilibrium

A profile \(s^{*} = (s_1^{*}, \dots, s_n^{*})\) is a Nash equilibrium if for every player \(i\) and every alternative \(s_i\):

\[ u_i(s_i^{*},\, s_{-i}^{*}) \;\ge\; u_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}^{*}). \]

Equivalently: \(s_i^{*} \in BR_i(s_{-i}^{*})\) for every \(i\) - every player's strategy is a best response to the others' strategies.

Three readings help. As a stability condition: if the profile were somehow reached, nobody has a private reason to move away. As a consistency of beliefs condition: each player is optimising against a correct forecast of the others. As a self-enforcing agreement: if the players could talk before playing but could not sign a binding contract, only a Nash profile could survive the conversation - any other suggestion contains at least one player with a private incentive to break the deal.

2. Finding pure equilibria in a matrix: the underline method

For each column, underline the row player's largest payoff; for each row, underline the column player's largest. A cell with both numbers underlined is a Nash equilibrium in pure strategies. The procedure is nothing more than the definition applied cell by cell, but doing it mechanically prevents mistakes.

Check (Opera, Opera): Alex switching to Football drops from 2 to 0; Bailey switching drops from 1 to 0. Neither deviates, so it is an equilibrium. The same argument works at (Football, Football). The off-diagonal cells are not equilibria: at (Opera, Football) both get 0 and either could improve alone.

Careful

Multiplicity is the norm, not the exception. Battle of the Sexes has two pure equilibria (and a third in mixed strategies, Chapter 4). Nash equilibrium tells you which profiles are stable; it does not tell you which one gets played. That gap is filled by conventions, communication, focal points (Schelling), or refinements such as subgame perfection.

3. Equilibrium need not be efficient

The Prisoner's Dilemma has the unique Nash equilibrium (Defect, Defect) with payoffs (1, 1), while (3, 3) is available. So a Nash equilibrium can be Pareto dominated - there can be another outcome that every player strictly prefers. Nash equilibrium is a positive concept (what will happen), not a normative one (what should happen). Much of applied economics is the study of institutions that change the game so that its equilibrium becomes efficient.

4. Continuous strategies: the Cournot duopoly

Strategies need not be finite. Two firms simultaneously choose quantities \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) of an identical good. The market price falls with total output: \(P = a - (q_1 + q_2)\). Each firm has constant unit cost \(c\). Firm 1's profit is

\[ \pi_1(q_1, q_2) = \bigl[\, a - q_1 - q_2 - c \,\bigr] \, q_1. \]

Differentiate with respect to \(q_1\), holding \(q_2\) fixed - this is exactly the 'best response' operation - and set the derivative to zero: \(\partial \pi_1 / \partial q_1 = a - 2q_1 - q_2 - c = 0\), giving the best-response function

\[ q_1 = \frac{a - c - q_2}{2} \qquad \text{and symmetrically} \qquad q_2 = \frac{a - c - q_1}{2}. \]

A Nash equilibrium is a point where the two best-response lines cross - each firm is optimising against what the other is actually doing. Solving the pair by symmetry gives

Cournot-Nash equilibrium
\[ q_1^{*} = q_2^{*} = \frac{a - c}{3}. \]

Take \(a = 100\) and \(c = 10\). Each firm produces 30, total output is 60, the price is 40 and each firm earns \((40 - 10) \times 30 = \mathbf{900}\). Now compare with collusion: a monopolist would produce \((a - c)/2 = 45\) in total, sell at 55, and earn 2025, i.e. 1012.5 per firm if the cartel split output. Competition destroys 112.5 of profit per firm - and the cartel is not a Nash equilibrium, because at a total of 45 each firm's best response is to expand. Cournot is a Prisoner's Dilemma with continuous strategies.

5. What Nash equilibrium assumes, and what it does not

The concept assumes each player's belief about the others is correct. That is a strong assumption. It is plausible when the game has been played many times and behaviour has settled down, when there is a public convention, or when the game is dominance solvable so that beliefs are pinned by reasoning alone. It is far less plausible in a novel one-shot interaction, and this is why rationalizability (Chapter 2) is sometimes the more honest prediction.

Nash equilibrium also does not assume players cooperate, communicate or randomise jointly. It only requires unilateral stability. Profiles that are stable against group deviations - the strong equilibrium and the core - are studied in Chapter 10.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Nash equilibriumA strategy profile in which each player's strategy is a best response to the others'.
Best-response functionThe map from opponents' strategies to the set of strategies maximising a player's payoff.
Pareto dominanceOutcome A Pareto dominates B if every player weakly prefers A and at least one strictly prefers it.
Focal pointAn equilibrium selected by shared cultural or contextual salience rather than by payoffs (Schelling).
Cournot competitionSimultaneous quantity choice by firms facing a downward-sloping inverse demand curve.
Coordination gameA game with several Nash equilibria in which players gain from matching each other's choice.

Check your understanding

  1. Is (Opera, Football) in Battle of the Sexes an equilibrium? (Answer: No - both get 0 and either alone can improve)
  2. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, is the unique Nash equilibrium Pareto efficient? (Answer: No - (3,3) is better for both)
  3. With \(a = 100\) and \(c = 10\), what is each Cournot firm's equilibrium output? (Answer: \(\tfrac{100-10}{3} = 30\))
  4. Why is the joint-monopoly output split not a Nash equilibrium in Cournot? (Answer: Each firm's best response to the low total is to expand)
