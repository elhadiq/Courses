Zero-Sum Games and the Minimax Theorem

Pure conflict, security levels, the value of a game, and its link to linear programming.

What you will be able to do
  • Recognise a zero-sum game and write it with a single payoff matrix.
  • Compute maximin and minimax values and locate a saddle point when one exists.
  • State the minimax theorem and its consequences: unique value and interchangeable equilibria.
  • Solve a 2x2 zero-sum game by equalising, obtaining both optimal mixtures and the value.
  • Explain why zero-sum equilibria are computable by linear programming.

1. Pure conflict

A two-player game is zero-sum (or strictly competitive) if the payoffs in every cell add to zero: \(u_2(s) = -u_1(s)\) for all \(s\). Nothing is lost by adding a constant, so 'constant-sum' games behave identically. In such games there is nothing to negotiate: every gain of mine is exactly your loss. Poker between two players, penalty kicks, and the classical model of a military engagement are the standard examples. Because one payoff table determines everything, we write only player 1's payoffs and call player 1 the maximiser and player 2 the minimiser.

2. Security levels: maximin and minimax

Write \(a_{ij}\) for player 1's payoff in row \(i\), column \(j\). Ask a cautious question: if I choose row \(i\), what is the worst that can happen to me? The worst is \(\min_j a_{ij}\). The best I can guarantee, whatever the opponent does, is the largest of these minima.

\[ \underline{v} \;=\; \max_i \min_j a_{ij} \qquad\text{(maximin, player 1's security level)} \]
\[ \overline{v} \;=\; \min_j \max_i a_{ij} \qquad\text{(minimax, player 2's security level)} \]
\[ \text{and always}\quad \underline{v} \;\le\; \overline{v}. \]

The inequality is easy to see and worth internalising: the player who must commit second has an advantage, so being able to react cannot hurt. When the two coincide, the common value is called the value of the game, and the cell that achieves it is a saddle point.

3. The minimax theorem

Most zero-sum games have no saddle point in pure strategies - Matching Pennies has \(\underline{v} = -1\) and \(\overline{v} = +1\). Von Neumann's theorem (1928) says that the gap always closes once mixing is allowed.

Theorem — minimax (von Neumann, 1928)

For every finite two-player zero-sum game,

\[ \max_{\sigma_1 \in \Delta(S_1)} \min_{\sigma_2 \in \Delta(S_2)} u_1(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) \;=\; \min_{\sigma_2 \in \Delta(S_2)} \max_{\sigma_1 \in \Delta(S_1)} u_1(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) \;=\; v. \]

The number \(v\) is the value of the game; each player has an optimal mixed strategy guaranteeing it.

This is the sharpest result in the subject, and it predates Nash by more than twenty years. Its consequences are strong. In a zero-sum game all Nash equilibria give the same payoffs, namely \(v\); equilibrium strategies are interchangeable, so if \((\sigma, \tau)\) and \((\sigma', \tau')\) are both equilibria then so are \((\sigma, \tau')\) and \((\sigma', \tau)\); and there is no coordination problem at all. None of these properties survive outside the zero-sum world - which is exactly why non-zero-sum games are harder.

4. Solving a 2x2 zero-sum game

Let player 1 play Top with probability \(p\). Player 2 will play whichever column is worse for player 1, so player 1 chooses \(p\) to equalise the two columns:

\[ \underbrace{3p - 2(1-p)}_{\text{against Left}} = \underbrace{-p + 4(1-p)}_{\text{against Right}} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; 5p - 2 = 4 - 5p \;\Longleftrightarrow\; p = 0.6, \]
\[ \text{guaranteeing } \; 3(0.6) - 2(0.4) = 1. \]

Let player 2 play Left with probability \(q\), chosen to equalise player 1's two rows: \(3q - (1-q) = -2q + 4(1-q)\), that is \(4q - 1 = 4 - 6q\), giving \(q = 0.5\), and player 1's payoff is again \(1\). The two computations agreeing at \(1\) is the minimax theorem in action: the value of the game is \(\mathbf{v = 1}\), favouring player 1.

5. Linear programming and duality

Finding the value is a linear program. Player 1 solves

\[ \max_{p,\,v} \; v \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_i p_i a_{ij} \ge v \;\; \forall j, \qquad \sum_i p_i = 1, \qquad p_i \ge 0, \]
\[ \text{while player 2 solves} \quad \min_{q,\,w} \; w \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_j q_j a_{ij} \le w \;\; \forall i, \qquad \sum_j q_j = 1, \qquad q_j \ge 0. \]

The second problem is exactly the dual of the first. The minimax theorem is therefore equivalent to LP strong duality, and any LP solver computes the value and optimal strategies in polynomial time. This is a striking contrast with general non-zero-sum games, where equilibrium computation is PPAD-complete: pure conflict is computationally easy; mixed motives are hard.

6. A caution about applying zero-sum thinking

Careful

Real interactions are rarely zero-sum, and treating them as if they were is a well-documented error. Trade, negotiation, and most workplace conflicts contain joint gains that the zero-sum frame makes invisible. Before importing the minimax machinery, check that payoffs really do sum to a constant in every cell.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Zero-sum gameA two-player game whose payoffs sum to zero (or a constant) in every cell.
Security levelThe payoff a player can guarantee regardless of the opponent's choice.
Maximin\(\underline{v} = \max_i \min_j a_{ij}\); player 1's security level.
Minimax\(\overline{v} = \min_j \max_i a_{ij}\); player 2's security level.
Saddle pointA cell that is simultaneously the minimum of its row and the maximum of its column.
Value of the gameThe common maximin and minimax payoff guaranteed by the minimax theorem.
InterchangeabilityIn zero-sum games, mixing and matching optimal strategies from different equilibria still gives an equilibrium.

Check your understanding

  1. What is always true of the maximin compared to the minimax? (Answer: \(\underline{v} \le \overline{v}\))
  2. In the 3x3 example, what is the value and where is the saddle point? (Answer: \(v = 3\), at \((A, Y)\))
  3. In the 2x2 example, what is \(p\) and what is the value? (Answer: \(p = 0.6\) and \(v = 1\))
  4. Why is a zero-sum equilibrium computationally easy to find? (Answer: It is a linear program, and the minimax theorem is LP duality)
