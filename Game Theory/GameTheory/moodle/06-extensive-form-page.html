Extensive-Form Games, Backward Induction and Subgame Perfection

Put timing back into the model, and throw out equilibria built on empty threats.

What you will be able to do
  • Draw and read a game tree with decision nodes, actions, payoffs and information sets.
  • Convert a small extensive-form game to its strategic form and find all Nash equilibria.
  • Apply backward induction and identify the subgame perfect equilibrium.
  • Explain non-credible threats and why commitment devices have value.
  • Compute the Stackelberg leader and follower quantities and compare them with Cournot.

1. Order matters

The strategic form throws away timing. Yet in most real interactions someone moves first and the other sees it: a firm builds a plant before a rival responds, a parent makes a threat before the child misbehaves, a bidder raises before others call. The extensive form keeps the timing by describing the game as a tree.

A game tree has decision nodes labelled with whose turn it is, branches for the available actions, terminal nodes carrying a payoff vector, and information sets grouping nodes a player cannot tell apart. If every information set is a single node, the game has perfect information: everyone always knows exactly where they are. Chess has perfect information; poker does not.

2. A worked tree: entry deterrence

An entrant decides whether to enter a market. If it stays out, it earns 0 and the incumbent keeps a monopoly worth 10. If it enters, the incumbent chooses to fight a price war (\(-2\) for the entrant, \(-2\) for the incumbent) or accommodate (\(2\) and \(5\)).

Now translate to the strategic form. The entrant has two strategies, In and Out. The incumbent also has two - Fight and Accommodate - even though it may never get to move; recall from Chapter 1 that a strategy specifies behaviour at every decision point.

Two pure Nash equilibria appear: (In, Accommodate) and (Out, Fight). The second is odd. It rests on the entrant believing a threat to fight - but if entry actually happened, fighting gives the incumbent \(-2\) and accommodating gives \(5\). The threat is not credible. Nash equilibrium cannot see this, because it never asks what would happen at a node that is not reached.

3. Backward induction

The cure is to solve the tree from the leaves back. At each decision node, assume the player there will choose optimally given what follows, replace the node by that payoff, and repeat.

In our tree: at the incumbent's node, \(5 \gt -2\), so Accommodate. Fold that back - entering now yields 2 for the entrant, staying out yields 0 - so the entrant enters. The backward-induction solution is (In, Accommodate) with payoffs (2, 5), and the equilibrium built on an empty threat is eliminated.

Theorem — Zermelo (1913)

Every finite game of perfect information has a solution by backward induction, hence a pure-strategy equilibrium. Applied to chess: from the opening position, either White can force a win, or Black can, or both can force at least a draw. Which one is true is unknown - the theorem asserts existence, not computability.

4. Subgame perfect Nash equilibrium

Backward induction generalises to trees with imperfect information through the notion of a subgame: a node together with everything that follows it, provided the node is alone in its information set and no information set is cut in half.

Definition - SPNE

A strategy profile is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE) if it induces a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, including those never reached in play. (Selten, 1965.)

Every SPNE is a Nash equilibrium; the converse fails, as (Out, Fight) shows. Subgame perfection is the first and most important refinement: it discards equilibria sustained by non-credible threats or promises. The practical lesson - the one that carries into negotiation, deterrence and monetary policy - is that a threat is only useful if carrying it out is in your interest at the time. That is why commitment is valuable: burning your ships, signing a penalty clause, or delegating to a stubborn agent changes the payoffs at the future node and thereby makes the threat credible.

5. First-mover advantage: Stackelberg competition

Return to the Cournot duopoly with \(P = a - Q\) and unit cost \(c\), but let firm 1 choose its quantity first and firm 2 observe it before choosing. Firm 2's best response is unchanged: \(q_2 = (a - c - q_1)/2\). Firm 1 therefore maximises \(\pi_1 = \bigl[a - q_1 - \tfrac{a - c - q_1}{2} - c\bigr] q_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}(a - c - q_1) q_1\), and the solution is

\[ q_1^{*} = \frac{a - c}{2} \;\; \text{(leader)}, \qquad q_2^{*} = \frac{a - c}{4} \;\; \text{(follower)}. \]

With \(a = 100\) and \(c = 10\): the leader produces 45, the follower 22.5, total 67.5 and the price is 32.5. Profits are 1012.5 for the leader and 506.25 for the follower, against 900 each under Cournot. Moving first is worth 112.5 here - not because the leader is cleverer, but because committing to a large quantity forces the follower to shrink. Note the contrast with Bertrand competition, where firms set prices and the follower can undercut, so moving first is a disadvantage. Whether commitment helps depends on whether the rival's best response goes down or up with your move.

6. Where backward induction strains

The centipede game is the standard stress test. Two players alternate; at each turn a player may Take a growing pot (ending the game) or Pass, which enlarges the pot but hands the choice over. Backward induction says: the last player would Take, so the second-to-last should Take, and so on back to the first move - the game ends immediately, with both players receiving far less than they could have. In laboratory play, most people pass for several rounds and earn more. The gap tells us something real: backward induction requires common knowledge of rationality to hold even at nodes whose very existence would be evidence that someone is not maximising in the assumed way.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Extensive formA description of a game as a tree: nodes, actions, information sets and terminal payoffs.
Perfect informationEvery information set is a singleton, so each player always knows the full history.
Information setA collection of nodes a player cannot distinguish when called on to move.
SubgameA node whose information set is a singleton, together with everything that follows it.
Backward inductionSolving a finite perfect-information tree from the terminal nodes back to the root.
SPNEA profile that is a Nash equilibrium in every subgame; rules out non-credible threats.
CommitmentAn irreversible action that changes one's own future payoffs so as to make a threat or promise credible.
Stackelberg competitionQuantity competition in which one firm chooses first and the other observes before choosing.

Check your understanding

  1. Why is (Out, Fight) a Nash equilibrium but not subgame perfect? (Answer: Fighting is not optimal at the node where it would be used)
  2. How many strategies does the incumbent have in the entry game? (Answer: Two - a strategy must specify play even at nodes that may not be reached)
  3. With \(a = 100\) and \(c = 10\), what is the Stackelberg leader's quantity? (Answer: \(\tfrac{100-10}{2} = 45\))
  4. What does Zermelo's theorem say about chess? (Answer: One of the three outcomes can be forced; it does not say which)
