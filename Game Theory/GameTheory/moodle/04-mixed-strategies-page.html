Mixed Strategies and Nash's Existence Theorem

Randomise, compute equilibria by indifference, and see why an equilibrium always exists.

What you will be able to do
  • Define a mixed strategy and compute expected payoffs against a mixture.
  • Apply the indifference principle to solve 2x2 games for their mixed equilibria.
  • Explain why each player's probabilities are set to make the opponent indifferent.
  • State Nash's existence theorem and outline the fixed-point argument behind it.
  • Distinguish the literal, population and beliefs interpretations of mixing.

1. Why randomise at all

Matching Pennies has no pure equilibrium. Whatever cell you propose, one player wants to move, and then the other wants to move, and you cycle forever. Something is missing from our strategy sets. What is missing is the option to be unpredictable. A tennis player who always serves wide will be broken; a tax authority that always audits the same profession will be gamed. Unpredictability is not a failure of decision-making, it is the decision.

Definition - mixed strategy

A mixed strategy \(\sigma_i \in \Delta(S_i)\) is a probability distribution over player \(i\)'s pure strategies. Payoffs are extended by expectation:

\[ u_i(\sigma) \;=\; \sum_{s \in S} \Bigl( \prod_{j} \sigma_j(s_j) \Bigr) u_i(s). \]

A pure strategy is the special case \(\sigma_i(s_i) = 1\) for one action.

2. The indifference principle - the one computational tool you need

Suppose in equilibrium a player puts strictly positive probability on two pure strategies, say A and B. If A gave a strictly higher expected payoff than B, the player would gain by shifting all the weight from B to A - so the profile would not be an equilibrium. Therefore:

Indifference principle

If \(\sigma^{*}\) is a Nash equilibrium, then for every player \(i\)

\[ u_i(s_i, \sigma_{-i}^{*}) = u_i(\sigma_i^{*}, \sigma_{-i}^{*}) \;\;\text{ for all } s_i \in \operatorname{supp}(\sigma_i^{*}), \qquad u_i(s_i, \sigma_{-i}^{*}) \le u_i(\sigma_i^{*}, \sigma_{-i}^{*}) \;\;\text{ otherwise.} \]

Every pure strategy played with positive probability yields the same expected payoff, and no unused pure strategy yields more.

Careful

The consequence is the fact students find most counterintuitive, and it is worth pausing on: each player's mixing probabilities are chosen to make the *opponent* indifferent, not themselves. You randomise in the exact proportions that leave your rival with no reason to prefer one reply over another.

3. Worked example: Matching Pennies

Let player 1 play Heads with probability \(p\). Player 2's expected payoffs are

\[ u_2(\text{Heads}) = -p + (1-p) = 1 - 2p, \qquad u_2(\text{Tails}) = p - (1-p) = 2p - 1. \]

Setting them equal gives \(1 - 2p = 2p - 1\), so \(\;\mathbf{p = \tfrac{1}{2}}\). By symmetry player 2 also plays Heads with probability \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), and the equilibrium value to each player is \(0\). The equilibrium is unique.

4. Worked example: Battle of the Sexes

Let Alex play Opera with probability \(p\) and Bailey play Opera with probability \(q\).

Make Bailey indifferent:

\[ \underbrace{p \cdot 1 + (1-p)\cdot 0}_{\text{Opera}} = \underbrace{p \cdot 0 + (1-p)\cdot 2}_{\text{Football}} \;\Longrightarrow\; p = 2 - 2p \;\Longrightarrow\; p = \tfrac{2}{3}. \]

Make Alex indifferent:

\[ \underbrace{2q}_{\text{Opera}} = \underbrace{1 - q}_{\text{Football}} \;\Longrightarrow\; q = \tfrac{1}{3}. \]

So the mixed equilibrium is \(\bigl(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3}\bigr)\) on Opera for Alex and Bailey respectively. Alex's expected payoff is \(2q = \tfrac{2}{3}\) and Bailey's is \(p = \tfrac{2}{3}\) - worse for both than either pure equilibrium, because with probability \(\tfrac{2}{3}\cdot\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot\tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{5}{9}\) the two end up in different places and get nothing. Adding mixed strategies gives this game three equilibria in total.

5. Nash's existence theorem

Theorem — existence (Nash, 1950)

Every finite game - finitely many players, each with finitely many pure strategies - has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies.

The proof is a fixed-point argument. Collect all players' mixed strategies into one profile \(\sigma \in \Delta = \prod_i \Delta(S_i)\), a compact convex set. Define the best-response correspondence \(BR(\sigma) = \prod_i \arg\max_{\sigma_i'} u_i(\sigma_i', \sigma_{-i})\). Expected payoffs are linear in each player's own probabilities, so \(BR(\sigma)\) is non-empty, convex-valued and has a closed graph. Kakutani's fixed-point theorem then yields a \(\sigma^{*}\) with \(\sigma^{*} \in BR(\sigma^{*})\) - and a fixed point of the best-response correspondence is precisely a Nash equilibrium. Convexity is where mixing earns its keep: with only pure strategies the best-response set can jump, and no fixed point need exist. Matching Pennies is exactly that failure.

Two cautions. The theorem guarantees existence, not uniqueness, and not that the equilibrium is easy to find: computing a Nash equilibrium of a two-player game is PPAD-complete (Daskalakis, Goldberg and Papadimitriou, 2009), so no efficient general algorithm is known. Infinite games need extra structure - continuous payoffs and compact convex strategy sets, as in Glicksberg's theorem - and can otherwise fail to have equilibria.

6. Reading a mixed equilibrium honestly

Three interpretations coexist and it is worth knowing which one you mean. Literal randomisation: the player really does flip a weighted coin - a natural reading in poker or in tax auditing. Population interpretation: a large population of players each playing a pure strategy, with 2/3 of them choosing Opera; an opponent drawn at random faces the mixture. Beliefs interpretation (Harsanyi): the probabilities describe the opponent's uncertainty about a player who is in fact acting purely, given tiny unobserved variations in payoffs. The last reading dissolves much of the discomfort with the indifference condition.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Mixed strategyA probability distribution \(\sigma_i \in \Delta(S_i)\) over a player's pure strategies.
SupportThe set of pure strategies assigned strictly positive probability by a mixed strategy.
Indifference principleIn equilibrium, all pure strategies in the support give equal expected payoff, and none outside gives more.
Expected payoffThe probability-weighted average of payoffs over all strategy profiles that may occur.
Kakutani fixed-point theoremA set-valued fixed-point theorem; the engine of Nash's existence proof.
PPAD-completeA complexity class capturing the difficulty of computing Nash equilibria; no polynomial algorithm is known.

Check your understanding

  1. In Matching Pennies, what probability does each player place on Heads? (Answer: \(p = \tfrac{1}{2}\), giving each an expected payoff of \(0\))
  2. In Battle of the Sexes, whose indifference determines Alex's probability \(p\)? (Answer: Bailey's - you set \(p\) so that Bailey is indifferent)
  3. Why do mixed equilibria always exist in finite games? (Answer: Mixing convexifies best-response sets, so Kakutani's theorem applies)
  4. Is the mixed equilibrium of Battle of the Sexes better or worse for the players than the pure ones? (Answer: Worse: 2/3 each instead of 2 and 1)
