Introduction: What Is Bitcoin?

Start here even if you have never touched cryptocurrency. By the end of this page you will understand what Bitcoin is, why it was invented, how its money is measured, and where its coins come from.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain, in one sentence, the problem Bitcoin was built to solve.
  • Describe the three things the word “Bitcoin” can mean.
  • Convert between bitcoin (BTC) and its smallest unit, the satoshi.
  • Explain why there will only ever be about 21 million bitcoin.
  • Recall the key dates in Bitcoin's early history.

1. The problem: sending money over the internet

Imagine you want to send a photo to a friend. You email it, and it just works — but notice something: you still have the photo too. Digital things are trivial to copy. That is wonderful for photos and terrible for money. If money were just a file, you could send the same “coin” to two people at once. This is called the double-spending problem.

For decades the only fix was to put a trusted middleman in charge — a bank or a company like PayPal. They keep a single master list (a ledger) of who owns what. When you pay someone, the bank subtracts from your row and adds to theirs. Because everyone trusts the bank's copy of the ledger, the same money cannot be spent twice.

The big idea

Bitcoin, first described in 2008, solved the double-spending problem without any trusted middleman. Instead of one bank holding the ledger, thousands of computers around the world each hold an identical copy and agree on its contents using cryptography and a clever competition called mining (Section 12). No single person, company, or country is in charge.

2. Three meanings of one word

People use “Bitcoin” to mean three related-but-different things. Keeping them straight will save you a lot of confusion later.

The word refers to… Meaning
bitcoin, the currency The units of value that people send and receive (written lowercase, or abbreviated BTC).
Bitcoin, the protocol The set of rules — the “rulebook” — that says what a valid transaction and a valid block look like.
Bitcoin, the network The worldwide collection of computers (nodes) running that rulebook and passing messages to each other.

So a full sentence might be: “I sent 0.01 bitcoin (currency) in a transaction that followed the Bitcoin protocol (rules) and was relayed across the Bitcoin network (computers).”

3. How a payment works, in one picture

You do not need the details yet — later sections cover each step — but here is the whole journey of a single payment so the rest of the course has a map to hang on.

In plain words: you build a message saying “pay this person,” you prove it is really you with a secret private key (Section 4), you send it out to the network, miners bundle it with other payments into a block, and once that block is accepted the payment is confirmed and practically impossible to reverse.

In practice you never see any of this machinery — a wallet app hides it behind two simple screens. To receive, you show a request (usually a QR code); to send, you enter an amount and a destination:

4. Units: bitcoin and satoshis

Just as one dollar is 100 cents, one bitcoin is divided into smaller units. The smallest unit is called a satoshi (named after Bitcoin's pseudonymous creator). There are 100,000,000 satoshis in one bitcoin — that is 108, a hundred million.

The rule to memorise

1 BTC = 100,000,000 satoshis   (1 satoshi = 0.00000001 BTC)

Why bother with such a tiny unit? Because computers should never store money as decimals — rounding errors in fractions like 0.1 can make amounts drift. So Bitcoin software counts everything in whole satoshis (integers) and only shows you BTC for readability. You will use this idea constantly in the programming exercises.

5. Where do bitcoins come from? The supply schedule

New bitcoins are not printed by a government. They are created as a reward paid to the miners who secure the network (Section 12). Each new block comes with a fixed reward called the block subsidy. Two facts make Bitcoin's money supply special:

  1. The subsidy started at 50 BTC per block when the network launched in 2009.
  2. Every 210,000 blocks — roughly every four years — the subsidy is cut in half. This event is called the halving.
Era Blocks Approx. year Subsidy per block
1st0 – 209,999200950 BTC
2nd210,000 – 419,999201225 BTC
3rd420,000 – 629,999201612.5 BTC
4th630,000 – 839,99920206.25 BTC
5th840,000 – …20243.125 BTC
………halves until it reaches 0

Because each era adds 210,000 × (a subsidy that keeps halving: 50, 25, 12.5, …), the running total is a converging geometric series. Add them all up and you get a number that gets closer and closer to — but never exceeds — 21 million bitcoin. This hard cap is why Bitcoin is often described as “digitally scarce” or compared to gold. (In the exercises you will compute this total exactly, in satoshis.)

Common misconception. The 21-million cap does not mean Bitcoin will “run out.” Once the subsidy fades, miners are paid through transaction fees instead (Section 9). And because each bitcoin splits into 100 million satoshis, there are plenty of units to go around.

6. A short history

  • 2008 — A paper titled “Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System” is published under the pseudonym Satoshi Nakamoto. (You can read it yourself — it is Appendix A of the book.)
  • January 2009 — The network launches. The very first block, block 0, is called the genesis block.
  • 2010 — The first real-world purchase: two pizzas paid for with 10,000 BTC, now a famous illustration of how far the currency has come.
  • 2010 onward — Satoshi hands off the project and disappears; development continues as open-source software maintained by a global community.

Bitcoin did not appear from nothing — it combined decades of earlier work in cryptography, digital cash, and distributed systems. Its breakthrough was putting those pieces together to remove the trusted middleman.
