Section 2 · Reading

How Bitcoin Works

We follow one real payment from start to finish. Along the way you will learn the single most important idea in Bitcoin: coins are not balances in an account — they are a chain of outputs.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a UTXO is and why Bitcoin uses “coins” instead of account balances.
  • Read a transaction as a set of inputs and outputs.
  • Compute the miner fee as inputs minus outputs.
  • Explain why wallets create a change output, and calculate it.
  • Describe what “mining” and a “confirmation” mean at a high level.

1. A story: Alice buys a coffee

Alice wants to buy a $5 coffee from Bob's café, which accepts bitcoin. Bob shows Alice a payment request (often a QR code). Alice's wallet app builds a transaction — a signed message that moves value from Alice to Bob — and sends it to the Bitcoin network. A few seconds later Bob sees it arrive; a few minutes later it is permanently recorded. That is the whole experience for a user. The rest of this page opens the hood to show what actually happened.

2. The key idea: coins are outputs, not balances

Here is where Bitcoin differs from a bank account, and where most beginners get stuck. Your bank has a single number — your balance — and adjusts it up and down. Bitcoin has no balances at all. Instead, the ledger is a giant pile of individual chunks of money called outputs.

Think of it like physical cash in your pocket. You do not have “a balance” — you have specific bills: a $20, a $5, two $1s. Each bill is a discrete object. When you receive bitcoin, you receive a specific chunk of a specific size. A chunk that you have received and not yet spent is called an Unspent Transaction Output, almost always shortened to UTXO.

Definition — UTXO

A UTXO is a piece of bitcoin you have received and can still spend. Your wallet “balance” is just the sum of all the UTXOs it controls. When you spend, you consume whole UTXOs — you cannot tear a bill in half.

3. Inputs and outputs

Every transaction has two sides:

  • Inputs — the UTXOs being spent. Each input points back to an earlier output and says “I am now using this chunk.” An input must be spent in full.
  • Outputs — the new chunks being created, each assigned to a recipient. Today's outputs become tomorrow's spendable UTXOs.

So a transaction destroys some old chunks and mints new ones of equal-or-lesser total value. A useful way to picture it is double-entry bookkeeping: inputs on one side, outputs on the other, and the small difference is the fee.

Transactions also chain together: the output of one becomes the input of the next, forming an unbroken record of ownership back through time.

Read the picture left to right: one input worth 100,000 sat comes in; two outputs go out (60,000 sat to Bob, 39,000 sat back to Alice); and 1,000 sat is left over. That leftover is the fee. Let us make the arithmetic explicit, because it is the heart of this section.

4. The fee is inputs minus outputs

A transaction never states its fee directly. The fee is simply whatever value is left over when you subtract the outputs from the inputs:

The fee formula

fee = (sum of inputs) − (sum of outputs)

fee = 100,000 − (60,000 + 39,000) = 1,000 sat

Whoever mines the block containing this transaction gets to keep that 1,000 sat. Fees are how you bid for space in a block (Section 9). A higher fee generally means faster confirmation.

Danger. Because the fee is just “whatever is left over,” a bug that forgets an output can accidentally hand a fortune to a miner. This is exactly why the next idea — change — matters so much.

5. Change: getting your money back

Remember that an input must be spent in full. Alice's only UTXO was 100,000 sat, but the coffee cost just 60,000 sat. She cannot hand over “part” of the chunk — so her wallet sends the whole 100,000 sat into the transaction and creates a second output paying the leftover back to an address Alice controls. That second output is called change, exactly like the coins you get back at a shop.

The change formula

change = (sum of inputs) − amount_paid − fee

change = 100,000 − 60,000 − 1,000 = 39,000 sat

If Alice's wallet had forgotten the change output, the fee formula would have made the fee 40,000 sat instead of 1,000 — a very expensive coffee. Wallets handle this automatically, but as a programmer you must always account for change yourself.

6. The one rule miners enforce: no money from nothing

A normal transaction may never create value out of thin air. The network rejects any transaction whose outputs add up to more than its inputs, and it rejects zero-or-negative output amounts. In short:

valid  ⇔  sum(outputs) ≤ sum(inputs)  AND  every output > 0

(The one exception is the special coinbase transaction that pays the miner the block subsidy — that is where brand-new coins legitimately appear, covered in Section 12.)

7. Mining and confirmation

Once Alice broadcasts her transaction, it travels from computer to computer and waits in each miner's mempool (a holding area of unconfirmed transactions). Miners compete to bundle waiting transactions into the next block. To win the right to add a block, a miner must solve a hard numeric puzzle called proof-of-work — essentially guessing numbers until a block's fingerprint falls below a target. This is deliberately expensive; it is what makes rewriting history impractical.

When a miner succeeds, the new block (including Alice's transaction) is shared with the whole network. Alice's payment now has one confirmation. Each additional block built on top adds another confirmation and makes reversal exponentially harder. Bob might accept the coffee at one confirmation; a bank-sized transfer might wait for six.
