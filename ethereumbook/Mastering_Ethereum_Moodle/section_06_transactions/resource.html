Section 6 · Reading

Transactions

By the end of this lesson you will know what a transaction contains, why the nonce matters, how gas fees are calculated under EIP-1559, and what the value and data fields do.

What you will be able to do

  • Name the five EIP-2718 transaction types and identify each by its type byte.
  • Explain the two jobs the nonce does: ordering and duplicate protection.
  • Compute a simple transfer fee and an EIP-1559 fee from base and priority fees.
  • Describe the value and data fields and what makes a transaction a contract call.

1. The shape of a transaction

A transaction is a signed message that requests a change to Ethereum's state. Since EIP-2718, every transaction begins with a single type byte so the network can tell different formats apart:

Type byteNameNote
0x00LegacyThe original format.
0x01EIP-2930Adds an access list.
0x02EIP-1559Base fee + priority fee (today's default).
0x03EIP-4844Blob transactions for rollups.
0x04EIP-7702Lets an EOA act with contract code.

Every transaction also carries a sender, a recipient, a value, a data payload, gas fields, a nonce, and a signature. We look at the most important of these next.

2. The nonce: order and no duplicates

The nonce is a counter of how many transactions an address has already sent. It is not stored directly; it is derived by counting confirmed transactions from that address. It quietly solves two problems.

Ordering. Suppose you sign a 6-ETH payment (nonce 3) then an 8-ETH payment (nonce 4), but your account holds only 10 ETH. Across a decentralized network, nodes may see them in either order. The nonce forces the network to process nonce 4 only after nonce 3, so the transaction you sent first wins.

Duplicate protection. Without a nonce, two payments of 2 ETH to the same address would look identical, and anyone could “replay” your transaction over and over to drain you. Because each transaction carries a unique, incrementing nonce, no two are ever identical — replay is impossible. This is why an account-based chain like Ethereum needs a nonce, unlike Bitcoin's UTXO model.

3. Gas and fees

Gas is the fuel that meters computation. It is not ether: it is a separate accounting unit priced in wei per gas, kept distinct so fee logic is insulated from ether's price swings. Two fields control it. The gas limit is the most gas you are willing to buy (like the capacity of a fuel tank). A plain transfer of ether between two EOAs always costs exactly 21,000 gas; a contract call cannot be known exactly in advance because the code may take different paths.

Since EIP-1559 the fee has two parts. The base fee is the minimum price per gas a block demands; it is burned. The priority fee (a tip) is added on top and goes to the validator that includes your transaction. So the total you pay is:

fee = gas_used × (base_fee + priority_fee)

A simple transfer: 21,000 × gas_price. At a price of 100 wei/gas that is 2,100,000 wei.

You must hold enough ether to cover the maximum (limit × max price) up front, but you are only billed for the gas actually consumed. Computing these fees is what the exercises drill.

4. Recipient, value, and data

The recipient (to) is a 20-byte address, either an EOA or a contract. Ethereum does not check that it corresponds to a real key or contract — sending to a mistyped address usually burns the ether forever, which is why address validation (the ERC-55 checksum from section 4) belongs in the user interface.

The payload lives in two fields, value (ether to transfer) and data (bytes). All four combinations are valid: value only, data only, both, or neither. When the recipient is a contract and the transaction carries data, that data selects which function to run and its arguments — this is how a transaction calls a contract. A special case with an empty to field creates a new contract.

Check your understanding

  1. Which type byte is an EIP-1559 transaction? (Answer: 0x02.)
  2. Name the nonce's two jobs. (Answer: ordering and duplicate protection.)
  3. How much gas does a plain EOA-to-EOA transfer use? (Answer: 21,000.)
  4. Where does the priority fee go? (Answer: to the validator that includes the transaction.)
