Zero-Knowledge Proofs

By the end of this lesson you will understand what a zero-knowledge proof is, its three defining properties, why it lets a verifier trust a computation without redoing it, and how SNARKs differ from STARKs.

What you will be able to do

  • Define prover, verifier, statement, witness, and proof.
  • State the three properties: completeness, soundness, and zero knowledge.
  • Explain why ZK proofs let Ethereum verify state without re-executing it.
  • Distinguish SNARKs (need a trusted setup) from STARKs (do not).

1. What is a zero-knowledge proof?

A zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) is a protocol that lets one party, the prover, convince another party, the verifier, that a statement is true — without revealing anything except that it is true. Three terms name the pieces:

TermMeaning
StatementThe public claim being proven.
WitnessThe secret information (known only to the prover) that makes it true.
ProofThe cryptographic object that convinces the verifier — without revealing the witness.

2. The three properties

Every ZK proof system must satisfy three properties:

PropertyGuarantee
CompletenessIf the statement is true, an honest prover can always produce a valid proof.
SoundnessA cheating prover cannot forge a valid proof for a false statement.
Zero knowledgeThe verifier learns nothing beyond the fact that the statement is true.

Matching each property to its short description is the first exercise.

3. Why Ethereum cares

The real power of ZK proofs for Ethereum is this: you can verify the validity of a computation without redoing it. A prover runs the heavy computation and attaches a proof; every verifier just checks the proof and trustlessly accepts the result.

That maps perfectly onto Ethereum's state-transition function. Today, every full node re-executes every transaction in a block to update its state. That re-execution is the bottleneck that forces the decentralization-vs-throughput trade-off. With ZK proofs, a few powerful nodes execute the block and produce a proof; all other nodes simply verify the proof — cheaply — and update their state. High throughput and low node requirements at the same time. (The catch, today, is that generating such a proof faster than re-execution is still hard — an optimization problem of “when, not if.”)

This is exactly why ZK rollups (from section 16) work: they prove a batch of L2 execution and post that proof to L1.

4. A concrete intuition: the partition problem

A classic warm-up is the partition problem: can a set of positive integers be split into two groups whose sums are equal? For S = [1, 1, 5, 7] the answer is yes — [1, 1, 5] and [7] both sum to 7. A verifier can check a proposed split cheaply: a partition is valid exactly when one subset's sum is half the total, i.e. twice the subset sum equals the total. Checking that condition is the second exercise, and it captures the essence of ZK verification: confirming a solution is far cheaper than finding one (the partition problem is NP-complete).

5. SNARKs vs STARKs

Two families of ZK proofs dominate. SNARKs are small and fast to verify but typically require a one-time trusted setup to generate secret parameters — if that setup is compromised, so is soundness. STARKs need no trusted setup and rely only on hashes (so they resist quantum attacks), at the cost of larger proofs. Both are used in ZK rollups. Deciding whether a system needs a trusted setup is the third exercise.

A caution. Bleeding-edge cryptography is risky: a subtle flaw in Zcash's original SNARK sat undiscovered in a peer-reviewed paper for years. ZK proofs are powerful, but “high security” should never be assumed without scrutiny.

Check your understanding

  1. Which property stops a cheat from proving a false statement? (Answer: soundness.)
  2. Why do ZK proofs help Ethereum scale? (Answer: nodes verify a proof instead of re-executing.)
  3. Is [1,1,5,7] a valid partition into equal halves? (Answer: yes — each half sums to 7.)
  4. Which system needs a trusted setup, SNARK or STARK? (Answer: SNARK.)
