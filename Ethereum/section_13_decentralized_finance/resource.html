Decentralized Finance

By the end of this lesson you will understand how automated market makers price trades with the constant-product formula, why that shape protects a pool, and how over-collateralized lending works.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how a DEX replaces the order book with a liquidity pool.
  • Use the constant-product formula x × y = k to read a pool's price and trade output.
  • Explain why the hyperbola shape stops a pool from being drained.
  • Describe over-collateralized lending and the role of the collateralization ratio.

1. DEXs and the AMM idea

DeFi (decentralized finance) rebuilds financial services — trading, lending, stablecoins — out of smart contracts, with no bank or broker in the middle. The flagship example is the decentralized exchange (DEX). Traditional exchanges use an order book that matches buyers and sellers, but on a slow, pay-per-action blockchain that never worked well.

The breakthrough was the automated market maker (AMM). Instead of matching orders, an AMM holds a liquidity pool of two tokens supplied by users, and a formula sets the price from the ratio of tokens in the pool. Anyone can supply liquidity and earn a share of the trading fees. Uniswap popularized the simplest such formula.

2. The constant-product formula

Uniswap V2's rule is x × y = k, where x and y are the amounts of the two tokens and k stays constant through every trade. The price of one token is simply the ratio of the reserves:

pool = 10 ETH + 100 USDC → price of 1 ETH = 100 ÷ 10 = 10 USDC
pool = 10 ETH + 200 USDC → price of 1 ETH = 200 ÷ 10 = 20 USDC

When someone trades, the amount they receive follows from keeping k constant:

Δy = (y × Δx) ÷ (x + Δx)

Here Δx is what you put in and Δy is what you get out. Computing the price and this trade output are the first two exercises.

3. Why the hyperbola protects the pool

The formula x × y = k traces a hyperbola — a curve that never touches either axis. That is the whole point. As you try to buy more and more of one token, its price climbs steeply, so it is mathematically impossible to drain either token to zero. A large trade relative to the pool gets a progressively worse rate, producing slippage (the gap between expected and actual price) that naturally discourages pool-draining trades.

A naive linear (fixed) price would be disastrous: whenever the fixed rate differed from the wider market, arbitrageurs would trade against the pool until one side ran dry. The self-protecting hyperbola is what made trustless, permissionless trading practical.

Impermanent loss. Providing liquidity is not risk-free: if the price of the pooled tokens moves a lot, your pool holdings can be worth less than if you had simply held the tokens — even after collecting fees.

4. Lending markets

A lending market lets users deposit crypto to earn interest and lets borrowers take loans — all through smart contracts, with rates set algorithmically by supply and demand. Because crypto is volatile, loans are over-collateralized: you must lock up collateral worth more than you borrow. A common collateralization ratio of 150% means borrowing $100 requires at least $150 of collateral.

required collateral = loan × ratio ÷ 100
loan 100 at 150% → 150 collateral

If the collateral's value falls below a threshold, the contract liquidates it — selling it to repay the loan and keep the pool solvent. DeFi relies on incentives here: liquidators are rewarded a slice of what they liquidate, so they watch the market for the protocol. Computing the required collateral is the third exercise.

Check your understanding

  1. A pool holds 10 ETH and 200 USDC. What is 1 ETH worth? (Answer: 20 USDC.)
  2. Why can't a constant-product pool be drained to zero? (Answer: the price rises steeply — the hyperbola never hits the axis.)

  4. Who triggers liquidations, and why? (Answer: incentivized users, for a reward.)
