Section 2 — Classification: Logistic Regression, k-NN and Support Vector Machines

From the Bayes-optimal rule to margins and kernels, with the loss functions and evaluation metrics that make classification behave differently from regression.

2.1 The Bayes classifier: the target we are chasing

In classification the output space is finite, Y = {1, …, K}. With the 0–1 loss \(L(y, \hat{y}) =1[y \ne \hat{y}]\), the expected risk is simply the probability of error, and the minimiser is known exactly.

Theorem — The Bayes rule is optimal
The classifier \(f*(x) = \operatorname{argmax}_{k} P(Y = k | X = x)\) minimises P(f(X) \(\ne Y\)) among all measurable functions. Its risk, the Bayes rate, is the irreducible error of the problem — the classification analogue of \(\sigma^2\) in Section 1.

Every method in this section is an attempt to approximate P(Y | X) or its argmax without knowing the distribution. They differ in what they model:

FamilyWhat is modelledExample
GenerativeP(X | Y) and P(Y), then Bayes' ruleLDA, naïve Bayes
Discriminative probabilisticP(Y | X) directlylogistic regression [3]
Discriminative non-probabilisticonly the decision boundaryperceptron, SVM [7]
Non-parametric / localno global model at allk-NN [2]

2.2 k-nearest neighbours: the simplest consistent learner

Fix and Hodges [1] proposed it in 1951; Cover and Hart [2] gave it its famous guarantee. To classify x, find the k training points nearest to x and take a majority vote. There is no training phase — the whole dataset is the model.

Theorem — Cover & Hart (1967)
As \(n \to \infty\), the asymptotic error rate \(R_{1NN}\) of the one-nearest-neighbour rule satisfies R* \(\le R_{1NN} \le 2R*(1 - R*) \le 2R*\), where R* is the Bayes rate. In words: with infinite data, half of the available information is carried by the single nearest neighbour alone.

k controls smoothness. k = 1 gives a jagged boundary with zero training error (each point is its own nearest neighbour) — maximal variance. Large k averages over a wide neighbourhood, smoothing the boundary and increasing bias. This is exactly the trade-off of Section 1, parameterised by 1/k rather than by degree.

The curse of dimensionality
In d dimensions, to capture a fraction r of the data volume a neighbourhood must span an edge length of \(r^{1/d}\) along each axis. For d = 10 and r = 1 %, that is 0.63 — 63 % of the range of every feature. "Nearest" neighbours in high dimension are not near, and all pairwise distances concentrate around the same value [11]. Distance-based methods need either few dimensions or a learned metric.

2.3 Logistic regression

Linear regression is a poor classifier: its output is unbounded and the squared loss penalises points that are confidently and correctly classified. Cox [3] instead models the log-odds as linear, which is equivalent to squashing a linear score through the logistic sigmoid \(\sigma\).

\[\sigma (z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z}) P(Y=1 | x) = \sigma (\theta^{T}\tilde{x}) \log[ p / (1-p) ] = \theta^{T}\tilde{x}\]

Parameters are fitted by maximum likelihood; equivalently, by minimising the average cross-entropy (also called log-loss or binomial deviance):

\[J(\theta ) = -(1/n) \sum_{i} [ y_{i} \log p_{i} + (1 - y_{i}) \log(1 - p_{i}) ], p_{i} = \sigma (\theta^{T}\tilde{x}_{i})\]
Property — a remarkably clean gradient
\(\nabla J(\theta ) = (1/n) X^{T}(p - y)\). This is formally identical to the least-squares gradient with the prediction replaced by the predicted probability. J is convex (the Hessian \(X^{T}diag(p_{i}(1-p_{i}))X\) is positive semi-definite), so gradient descent or Newton's method (IRLS) reaches the global optimum. There is, however, no closed form.
Perfect separation
If the two classes are linearly separable, the likelihood has no finite maximiser: scaling \(\theta\) by any factor \(\gt 1\) always improves it, so \(\| \theta \|\) diverges and the fitted probabilities saturate at 0 and 1. An L2 penalty (Section 1.5) removes the pathology and is the reason most libraries regularise logistic regression by default.

2.4 The perceptron and the notion of margin

Rosenblatt's perceptron [4] predates all of this. It predicts sign(\(\theta^{T}\tilde{x}\)) and, on every misclassified point, updates \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + y_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}\).

Theorem — Novikoff's mistake bound [5]
If the data are separable with geometric margin \(\gamma \gt 0\) and all points satisfy \(\| x_{i}\| \le R\), the perceptron makes at most (R/\(\gamma )^2\) mistakes, regardless of n or d. It converges — but to some separating hyperplane, not a distinguished one.

That last clause is the opening for support vector machines. When many hyperplanes separate the data, which should we prefer? The answer proposed by Boser, Guyon and Vapnik [6] is the one furthest from the data.

2.5 Support Vector Machines

Hard margin

Normalising so that the closest points satisfy |\(\theta^{T}\tilde{x}| = 1\), the geometric margin is 2/\(\| \theta \|\). Maximising it is a convex quadratic programme:

\[\min_{\theta} \tfrac{1}{2} \| \theta \|^2 \text{subject to } y_{i}(w^{T}x_{i} + b) \ge 1 \text{for all } i\]

Introducing multipliers \(\alpha_{i} \ge 0\) gives the Lagrangian dual, which is where the method becomes powerful:

\[\max_{\alpha} \sum_{i}\alpha_{i} - \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} \alpha_{i}\alpha_{j} y_{i}y_{j} \langle x_{i}, x_{j}\rangle s.t. \sum_{i}\alpha_{i}y_{i} = 0, \alpha_{i} \ge 0\]
Definition — Support vectors
The KKT complementary-slackness condition \(\alpha_{i}[y_{i}(w^{T}x_{i}+b) - 1] = 0\) forces \(\alpha_{i} = 0\) for every point strictly outside the margin. Only the points on the margin have \(\alpha_{i} \gt 0\) — these are the support vectors, and w = \(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}y_{i}x_{i}\) depends on nothing else. Delete every other training point and the solution is unchanged.

Soft margin and the hinge loss

Real data are rarely separable. Cortes and Vapnik [7] introduced slack variables \(\xi_{i} \ge 0\) permitting violations at a price C:

\[\min \tfrac{1}{2} \| w\|^2 + C \sum_{i}\xi_{i} s.t. y_{i}(w^{T}x_{i}+b) \ge 1 - \xi_{i}, \xi_{i} \ge 0\]

Eliminating the slacks shows that the soft-margin SVM is nothing but regularised ERM with the hinge loss — making the connection to Section 1 exact:

\[\min_{w,b} (1/n)\sum_{i} \max(0, 1 - y_{i}f(x_{i})) + \lambda \| w\|^2, \lambda = 1/(2nC)\]

Small C means a strong penalty on ‖w‖, hence a wide, tolerant margin (high bias); large C forces the margin to shrink until it fits the data (high variance). C is chosen by cross-validation.

Compare 0–1, hinge, logistic and squared loss on the same axes.

Figure. Surrogate losses as a function of the margin m = \(y\cdot f(x)\). All are convex upper bounds on the 0–1 loss, which is itself non-convex and NP-hard to minimise. Hinge is exactly zero once \(m \ge 1 (\)hence sparsity in the support vectors); log-loss is never zero (every point keeps pulling); squared loss penalises points that are correct with large margin — which is why it is the wrong loss for classification.

The kernel trick

The dual objective touches the data only through inner products \(\langle x_{i}, x_{j}\rangle\). Aizerman et al. [8] observed — and Boser et al. [6] exploited — that any symmetric positive semi-definite function K(x, x') is an inner product in some feature space by Mercer's theorem. Replace the dot product by K and the SVM becomes non-linear at no extra cost, never computing the feature map explicitly.

KernelK(x, x')Feature space
Linear\(\langle x, x'\rangle\)the input space itself
Polynomial degree p(\(\langle x, x'\rangle + c)^{p}\)all monomials up to degree p, finite dimension
RBF / Gaussianexp(\(-\gamma \| x - x'\|^2\))infinite-dimensional
Sigmoidtanh(\(\kappa \langle x, x'\rangle + c\))not PSD for all parameters — use with care

\(\gamma\) in the RBF kernel is a second complexity knob: large \(\gamma\) makes each support vector influential only in a tiny neighbourhood, which reproduces the k = 1 nearest-neighbour behaviour and overfits. C and \(\gamma\) must be tuned jointly, usually on a logarithmic grid [12].

Training the dual is O(\(n^2) to O(n^3\)) in memory and time, which is why Platt's SMO algorithm [9] — optimising two multipliers at a time in closed form — remains the standard solver. Kernel SVMs do not scale to millions of points; that regime belongs to linear SVMs, ensembles (Section 3) or neural networks.

def hinge_loss(y_true, scores):
    """Mean hinge loss. y_true entries are -1 or +1, scores are w.x + b."""
    return sum(max(0.0, 1.0 - y * s) for y, s in zip(y_true, scores)) / len(y_true)

def rbf_kernel(x, z, gamma):
    d2 = sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip(x, z))
    return 2.718281828459045 ** (-gamma * d2)

2.6 Evaluating a classifier

Accuracy is misleading whenever classes are imbalanced: on a dataset with 1 % positives, predicting "negative" always scores 99 %. The confusion matrix and the quantities derived from it are the honest report [10].

PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)Of the ones I flagged, how many were right?
Recall (TPR, sensitivity)TP / (TP + FN)Of the real positives, how many did I catch?
Specificity (TNR)TN / (TN + FP)Of the real negatives, how many did I leave alone?
\(F_1\)2PR / (P + R)Harmonic mean — both must be high

The ROC curve traces (FPR, TPR) as the decision threshold sweeps from +\(\inftyto-\infty\); AUC is threshold-free and invariant to class prior. Fawcett [10] cautions that under heavy imbalance the ROC curve looks deceptively good because FPR has a huge denominator — the precision–recall curve is the more informative diagnostic there.

Summary

  • The Bayes rule \(\operatorname{argmax}_{k} P(Y=k|x)\) is optimal; every algorithm approximates it differently.
  • k-NN is asymptotically within a factor 2 of Bayes [2] but collapses in high dimension.
  • Logistic regression models the log-odds linearly, is convex, and needs regularisation under separation [3].
  • The SVM picks the maximum-margin hyperplane; only support vectors matter, and the dual form admits kernels [7] [8].
  • Hinge, log and squared losses are convex surrogates for the intractable 0–1 loss; their shape explains each method's behaviour.
  • Report precision, recall and AUC — never accuracy alone [10].
