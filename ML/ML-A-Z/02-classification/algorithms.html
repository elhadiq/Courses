How to read this annex

Each algorithm is set out the same way: what it is handed, what it must hand back, then the steps in the order you would carry them out. A small example is worked through by hand so you can check your understanding against real numbers, and the closing boxes say why the method is correct, why it stops, and what it costs. Nothing here assumes you can read a program — the point is the reasoning.

k-nearest-neighbour classification

Lazy method \(\cdot\) no training phase at all

Label a new observation by looking at the labelled observations closest to it and taking a vote.

Input and output

Given. A labelled dataset, a new point to classify, and an odd number \(k\).

Produce. One class label for the new point.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Measure every distance. Compute the distance from the new point to every labelled observation. Compare squared distances and skip the square root — it changes no ordering and saves time.
  2. Take the k closest. Sort by distance and keep the first \(k\) observations.
  3. Vote. Count the labels among those \(k\). The label appearing most often wins.
  4. Break ties by a stated rule. With an even split, fall back on the single nearest neighbour, or on the more frequent class overall. The rule must be fixed in advance, otherwise results are not reproducible.
Worked by hand

Five labelled points on a line, classifying \(x = 4.2\) with \(k = 3\).

PointPositionLabelDistance to 4.2Among 3 closest?

B3.0red1.2yes

E9.0blue4.8no

The three closest are B (red), C (blue) and D (blue), so the vote is 2–1 for blue. With \(k = 1\) it would also be blue; with \(k = 5\) it would be blue 3–2. The answer can change with \(k\), which is exactly why \(k\) must be chosen on held-out data.

Why the answer is correct

No model is fitted, so there is nothing to be correct about beyond the vote itself. The guarantee is asymptotic: with unlimited data, the single-nearest-neighbour error is at most twice the best error any method could achieve.

Why it stops

One pass over the data plus a sort. Always finishes.

What it costs

Nothing to train, but every prediction costs a full pass: \(nd\) operations. Predictions get slower as you collect more data — the opposite of most methods.

Where it goes wrong
  • In more than a handful of dimensions, all points become roughly equidistant and 'nearest' stops meaning anything. Reduce dimensions first.
  • Inputs on different scales let the widest-ranging one dominate the distance. Standardise before measuring.
  • With imbalanced classes the majority swamps every vote. Weight votes by inverse distance, or rebalance.

Fitting a logistic model by gradient descent

Iterative method \(\cdot\) convex, so no local traps

Find coefficients that make the model's predicted probability high for the positives and low for the negatives.

Input and output

Given. A table of inputs with labels 0 or 1, a step size, and a number of rounds.

Produce. Coefficients defining a probability for any new input.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Score each observation. Combine its inputs with the current coefficients into a single number. Large positive means confidently class 1.
  2. Squash the score into a probability. Pass it through the S-shaped logistic curve, which maps any number into the range 0 to 1. Compute it in the numerically safe way for large negative scores, or it will overflow.
  3. Compare with the truth. Subtract the true label from the predicted probability. The result lies between −1 and 1 and says how wrong, and in which direction.
  4. Attribute the error to each input. For each input column, multiply these differences by the column entry by entry and average.
  5. Step and repeat. Move each coefficient against that quantity, scaled by the step size, and go round again.
Worked by hand

Notice how the update is identical in form to the one for straight-line fitting — only the meaning of the prediction has changed.

MethodPrediction isCorrection term
Straight-line fittinga raw numbertrue − predicted
Logistic fittinga probabilitytrue − predicted

That is not a coincidence. Both losses come from the same family, and in both the derivative reduces to the same simple difference.

Why the answer is correct

The objective is convex: it has a single lowest point and no other flat spots. Any downhill method therefore reaches the global best, unlike the tree and clustering methods later on.

Why it stops

Stops when you stop it. In one special case it never converges — see the pitfalls.

What it costs

One pass over the table per round, \(nd\) operations.

Where it goes wrong
  • If the two classes can be separated perfectly by a straight boundary, there is no best answer: making the coefficients larger always improves the fit, so they grow without limit and the probabilities saturate at exactly 0 and 1. Adding a penalty on the coefficients removes the pathology, which is why most implementations penalise by default.
  • Computing the logistic curve the naive way overflows for scores around −750 and below.

The maximum-margin separator

Optimisation \(\cdot\) solved by a specialised routine

Among all the straight boundaries that separate two classes, choose the one that leaves the widest empty corridor.

Input and output

Given. A labelled dataset with labels −1 and +1, and a tolerance \(C\) for points on the wrong side.

Produce. A boundary, and the short list of observations that determine it.

The procedure, step by step

  1. State the goal. Every candidate boundary has a margin: the distance from it to the closest observation. Among separating boundaries, we want the largest margin.
  2. Normalise the description. Fix the scale so that the closest points sit at a signed distance of exactly 1. Maximising the margin then becomes minimising the length of the coefficient vector — a smooth, convex objective.
  3. Allow violations at a price. Real data are rarely separable. Give each observation permission to sit inside the corridor, or on the wrong side, and charge \(C\) per unit of violation.
  4. Hand it to a solver. The result is a quadratic programme with linear constraints. It is solved by working on two observations at a time, each pair having a closed-form answer, cycling until no pair can be improved.
  5. Keep only what matters. When the solver finishes, most observations turn out to have no influence. Only those on or inside the corridor — the support vectors — define the boundary. Every other observation could be deleted without changing the answer.

Typically only a small fraction of the data are support vectors, which is what makes the fitted model compact.

Why the answer is correct

Maximum margin is not an aesthetic preference. A wide corridor means the boundary can be shifted a long way before any observation changes side, which is what generalisation to new data requires.

Why it stops

The solver stops when every pair satisfies the optimality conditions to within a tolerance.

What it costs

Between quadratic and cubic in the number of observations, because the solver reasons about pairs. This is the reason kernel methods do not scale to millions of rows.

Where it goes wrong
  • Small \(C\) gives a wide, forgiving corridor that underfits; large \(C\) forces the corridor to shrink until it fits the noise. Choose it on held-out data.
  • Inputs must be standardised: the corridor is measured in ordinary distance, so an input with a wide range dominates it.
Worth knowing

Because the solver only ever needs inner products between pairs of observations, any function measuring similarity between two points can be substituted for the inner product. The boundary then becomes curved in the original space at no extra cost — the kernel trick.

Building the ROC curve

Evaluation procedure \(\cdot\) sweeps a threshold

Show how a classifier's hit rate and false-alarm rate trade off as you become more or less willing to declare a positive.

Input and output

Given. A set of observations with true labels and predicted scores.

Produce. A curve, and the single number underneath it.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Sort by score, highest first. The most confidently positive observation goes at the top.
  2. Start at the origin. With the threshold above every score, nothing is flagged: no hits, no false alarms.
  3. Walk down the list. Lower the threshold past one observation at a time. If it was truly positive, step upwards; if truly negative, step to the right.
  4. Normalise the axes. Divide the vertical steps by the total number of positives and the horizontal ones by the total number of negatives, so both axes run 0 to 1.
  5. Measure the area. The area under the resulting staircase is the summary number. It equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative.
Worked by hand

Six observations. P marks a true positive, N a true negative.

The area under this staircase is 8/9. A perfect ranking would go straight up then straight across, giving 1; random ranking would follow the diagonal, giving 1/2.

Why the answer is correct

The curve depends only on the order of the scores, not their values. Any monotone rescaling of the scores leaves it unchanged.

Why it stops

One pass down a sorted list.

What it costs

Dominated by the sort.

Where it goes wrong
  • Under heavy imbalance the curve flatters. With 1 % positives, the false-alarm rate is divided by a very large number, so it stays low even when most flagged items are wrong. Plot precision against recall instead.
  • The area summarises every threshold at once, including thresholds you would never use. If you deploy at one operating point, report the numbers at that point too.
