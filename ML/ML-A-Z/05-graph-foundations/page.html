PART II — GRAPH-BASED MACHINE LEARNING

5.1 Why graphs need their own machine learning

Every method in Part I assumed the same thing: the data are n independent points in R\(^{d}\), arranged in a rectangular table. Enormously important datasets violate both halves of that assumption. Social networks, molecules, citation corpora, road systems, knowledge bases, protein interaction maps and recommendation logs are all relational: the samples are connected, and those connections carry most of the signal [4].

Two properties break the classical toolbox outright:

  • The samples are not i.i.d. A user's label depends on their neighbours' labels. Cross-validation by random splitting leaks information across the edges.
  • There is no canonical ordering of the nodes. A graph on n nodes has up to n! equivalent adjacency-matrix representations. Any function we learn must be permutation-equivariant, which rules out feeding the adjacency matrix to an MLP as if it were an image.
Definition — Graph
A graph G = (V, E) consists of a vertex set V with |V| = n and an edge set \(E \subseteq V \times V\) with |E| = m. G is undirected if (u,v) \(\in E \Leftrightarrow (v,u) \in E\), and weighted if each edge carries \(w_{uv} \gt 0\). In machine learning each node usually also carries a feature vector, collected in a matrix \(X \in\) R\(^{n\times d}\). The pair (A, X) — structure plus attributes — is the input to every algorithm in Part II.

5.2 Matrix representations

Adjacency matrix

\[A_{uv} = w_{uv} if (u,v) \in E, 0 otherwise\]

For an undirected graph A is symmetric, so all its eigenvalues are real. Real graphs are extremely sparse — m = O(n) rather than O(\(n^2\)) — so A is never stored densely; adjacency lists or CSR format are standard, and every algorithm below is designed to touch only the m non-zeros.

Theorem — Powers of A count walks
For any \(k \ge 1\), the entry (\(A^{k})_{uv}\) equals the number of walks of length exactly k from u to v. Proof by induction: the case k = 1 is the definition; and (\(A^{k+1})_{uv} = \sum_{z} (A^{k})_{uz}A_{zv}\) sums, over every intermediate node z, the walks of length k reaching z times the edges from z to v — which enumerates each walk of length k+1 exactly once. \(\square\)

This single fact underlies almost everything that follows: multiplying by A is one hop of propagation. PageRank iterates it (Section 7), and a k-layer graph neural network applies it k times (Section 8), which is why such a network sees exactly the k-hop neighbourhood of each node.

The degree of node u is \(d_{u} = \sum_{v}A_{uv}\), and D = diag(\(d_1\), …, \(d_{n}\)) is the degree matrix. Note \(\sum_{u}d_{u} = 2m\) for an unweighted undirected graph — the handshaking lemma.

The graph Laplacian

Kirchhoff introduced this matrix in 1847 [1] to analyse currents in electrical networks. It is the single most important object in graph-based learning.

\[\mathbf{L = D - A} (combinatorial / \text{unnormalised Laplacian })\]

Its power comes from one identity. For any vector \(f \in\) R\(^{n}\), interpreted as a signal on the nodes:

Theorem — The Laplacian quadratic form
\(f^{T}Lf =\tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{u,v} w_{uv}(f_{u} - f_{v})^2 = \sum_{(u,v)\in E} w_{uv}(f_{u} - f_{v})^2\).

Proof. \(f^{T}Lf =f^{T}Df- f^{T}Af =\sum_{u}d_{u}f_{u}^2 - \sum_{u,v}w_{uv}f_{u}f_{v}\). Writing \(\sum_{u}d_{u}f_{u}^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} (\sum_{u,v}w_{uv}f_{u}^2 + \sum_{u,v}w_{uv}f_{v}^2)\) and completing the square gives the result. \(\square\)

In words: \(f^{T}\)Lf measures how much f disagrees across edges. It is a smoothness penalty, computed in O(m) time, and it is the graph analogue of \(\int |\nabla f|^2\) on a continuous domain.

Three consequences follow immediately and are used constantly:

  1. L is positive semi-definite (the quadratic form is a sum of squares), so its eigenvalues are real and satisfy 0 = \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le\) … \(\le \lambda_{n}\).
  2. The all-ones vector 1 is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 0, since L1 = D1 − A1 = d − d = 0. A constant signal has no disagreement anywhere.
  3. The multiplicity of eigenvalue 0 equals the number of connected components [13]. Indeed \(f^{T}\)Lf = 0 forces \(f_{u} = f_{v}\) on every edge, i.e. f constant on each component; the indicator vectors of the components form a basis of the null space. This is the entire theoretical basis of spectral clustering, and Section 6 is essentially the perturbation theory of this statement.
Definition — Algebraic connectivity
\(\lambda_2\), the second-smallest eigenvalue, is called the Fiedler value [2]. It is \(\gt 0\) if and only if the graph is connected, and its magnitude quantifies how hard the graph is to cut in two: small \(\lambda_2\) means a bottleneck. Its eigenvector, the Fiedler vector, is the smoothest non-constant signal on the graph, and its sign pattern gives a bipartition — the subject of Section 6.

Normalised Laplacians

On graphs with heterogeneous degrees, L is dominated by the hubs: a node of degree 1000 contributes a thousand times more to the quadratic form than a node of degree 1. Two normalisations correct this [3]:

5.3 Graph signals and smoothness

Once L is in hand, a whole signal-processing vocabulary transfers to graphs [10]. Write L = \(U\Lambda U^{T}\) for its eigendecomposition. The eigenvectors \(u_{i}\) play the role of Fourier modes: \(u_1\) is constant (\(\lambda = 0\), zero frequency), and eigenvectors with large \(\lambda\) oscillate rapidly across edges. The graph Fourier transform of a signal is \(\hat{f} = U^{T}f\), and \(\lambda_{i}\) is the frequency of mode i.

Filtering means reweighting the modes: \(f_{out} =Ug(\Lambda )U^{T}f. A\) low-pass filter g (large at small \(\lambda\), small at large \(\lambda\)) smooths the signal over the graph. Two remarks that matter practically:

  • Computing U costs O(\(n^3\)) — hopeless beyond a few thousand nodes. Practical filters are therefore written as polynomials in L, which need only sparse matrix–vector products (O(m) each) and are automatically localised: a degree-k polynomial in L touches only the k-hop neighbourhood.
  • That observation is the entire derivation of the GCN in Section 8: it is a first-order polynomial filter, applied repeatedly.
Definition — Homophily
A graph is homophilous when connected nodes tend to share labels — "birds of a feather flock together" [9]. Formally, the edge homophily ratio is h = |{(u,v) \(\in E:y_{u} = y_{v}\}| / |E|\). Homophily is the assumption that justifies smoothing a label signal over the graph; on heterophilous graphs (h low — dating networks, predator–prey webs, fraud rings) naive smoothing actively destroys the signal, and standard GCNs underperform a plain MLP. Always measure h before choosing a model.

The oldest graph-based learner exploits homophily directly. Label propagation [11] fixes the labelled nodes and solves for the harmonic extension minimising \(f^{T}\)Lf on the unlabelled ones — each unknown value ends up the weighted average of its neighbours. It is a closed-form, training-free baseline that graph neural networks are surprisingly often unable to beat.

def laplacian_quadratic(edges, f):
    """f^T L f = sum over edges of (f_u - f_v)^2, in O(m). edges is a list of (u, v, w)."""
    return sum(w * (f[u] - f[v]) ** 2 for u, v, w in edges)


def label_propagation(adj, labels, unlabeled, n_iter=100):
    """Iterative harmonic solution: each unlabelled node averages its neighbours.
    adj maps node -> list of neighbours; labels maps node -> float (fixed for labelled)."""
    f = dict(labels)
    for u in unlabeled:
        f.setdefault(u, 0.0)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        for u in unlabeled:
            nb = adj[u]
            if nb:
                f[u] = sum(f[v] for v in nb) / len(nb)
    return f

The Erdős–Rényi model [8], in which each edge appears independently with probability p, is the standard null model. Its degree distribution is Binomial(n−1, p) — sharply concentrated, with no hubs — which is exactly why real networks, with their heavy tails, are so obviously not random [5].

Any aggregation scheme that sums over neighbours without normalising will be dominated by those hubs.

5.5 The three learning tasks on graphs

Transductive vs inductive
In the transductive setting the whole graph, including the test nodes and all edges, is available at training time; only the test labels are hidden. Spectral methods and the original GCN are transductive — adding a new node requires refitting. In the inductive setting the model must generalise to nodes or entire graphs never seen during training, which requires a function of the local neighbourhood rather than a per-node parameter. GraphSAGE (Section 8) was designed exactly for this [4].
Evaluation on graphs is easy to get wrong
A random train/test split of the nodes leaves test nodes connected to training nodes, so message passing legitimately propagates label information across the split — that is the point of the method, but it means your split is not independent and accuracy is not comparable to the i.i.d. case. For link prediction, edges held out for testing must be removed from the graph before training, or the model simply reads the answer off the adjacency matrix. Report results on the standard public splits whenever they exist [12].

Summary

  • Graph data breaks the i.i.d. assumption and demands permutation-equivariant models.
  • (\(A^{k})_{uv}\) counts walks of length k: multiplying by A is one hop of propagation.
  • L = D − A is PSD and \(f^{T}Lf =\sum_{edges}(f_{u} - f_{v})^2\) is a smoothness penalty computable in O(m).
  • dim ker L = number of connected components [13]; \(\lambda_2\) is the algebraic connectivity [2].
  • Normalising by degree (\(L_{sym}, L_{rw}\)) bounds the spectrum by 2 and tames hubs [3].
  • Homophily [9] is the assumption behind every smoothing method — measure it before trusting one.
  • Node-, edge- and graph-level tasks, in transductive or inductive settings, organise the rest of this course.
