Growing a decision tree

Greedy recursive method \(\cdot\) never reconsiders a choice

Split the data repeatedly on one input at a time, so that each final group is as pure as possible in its labels.

Input and output

Given. A labelled table and stopping rules (a maximum depth, a minimum group size).

Produce. A tree of yes/no questions ending in groups, each predicting one label.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Measure the impurity of the current group. A group where every label is the same has impurity zero. A group split evenly between two labels is at its worst. Add up, over the labels, the proportion times one minus that proportion.
  2. Propose every possible split. For each input, sort the distinct values and consider a cut halfway between each neighbouring pair. Each cut sends part of the group left and part right.
  3. Score each split by the impurity it removes. Compute the impurity of the two sides, weight each by the fraction of observations it received, and subtract the total from the parent's impurity. The weighting matters: a split that isolates one observation cannot score well however pure that observation is.
  4. Take the best and commit. Apply the highest-scoring split. It is never revisited, whatever happens further down.
  5. Recurse. Repeat on each side. Stop when the group is pure, too small, or at the depth limit; that group becomes a leaf predicting its majority label.

The cut at 3.5 separates the classes perfectly and is chosen. Had no cut been perfect, the greedy rule would still have taken the best available and moved on — which is why a single tree is unstable.

Why the answer is correct

There is no optimality guarantee. Finding the best possible tree is computationally intractable, so every practical method is this greedy heuristic. What the impurity measure does guarantee is that the chosen split never increases impurity.

Why it stops

Each split strictly reduces the number of observations in a group, and groups cannot shrink below one. The recursion therefore bottoms out.

What it costs

At each node, sorting each input dominates. Overall about \(n\,d \log n\) per level.

Where it goes wrong
  • Grown without limit, a tree isolates every observation, scores perfectly on the data it was built from, and is useless on anything else.
  • The impurity-based importance of an input is biased towards inputs with many distinct values, simply because they offer more cuts to choose from. Do not read it as a measure of relevance.

Cost-complexity pruning

Post-processing \(\cdot\) trades accuracy for simplicity

Cut a fully grown tree back to the size that actually helps on new data.

Input and output

Given. A fully grown tree and a penalty per leaf, \(\alpha\).

Produce. A pruned subtree.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Score the whole tree. Total impurity across the leaves, plus \(\alpha\) times the number of leaves. Larger \(\alpha\) buys simplicity at the cost of fit.
  2. Find the weakest link. For each internal node, ask how much the score would worsen per leaf removed if everything below it were collapsed into a single leaf. Find the node where that ratio is smallest.
  3. Collapse it. Turn that node into a leaf predicting the majority of what it holds.
  4. Repeat, recording as you go. Continue until only the root remains, keeping every intermediate tree. These trees are nested, each obtained from the last, so the whole family is produced in one sweep.
  5. Choose among them by cross-validation. Score each candidate on held-out data and keep the best.
Why the answer is correct

As the penalty grows, the optimal subtrees form a nested sequence: each is contained in the previous one. That is why the whole regularisation path can be computed once rather than re-solved for every penalty value.

Why it stops

Each round removes at least one internal node.

What it costs

Cheap compared with growing the tree.

Where it goes wrong
  • Choosing the penalty on the data used to grow the tree always picks zero — the unpruned tree fits it best by construction.

Random forest

Ensemble \(\cdot\) parallel, attacks variance

Average many deliberately different trees so that their individual instabilities cancel.

Input and output

Given. A labelled table, a number of trees \(B\), and a count \(m\) of inputs to offer at each split.

Produce. A committee of trees that vote.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Draw a bootstrap sample. Sample \(n\) observations with replacement. Some appear several times, and about 37 % do not appear at all.
  2. Grow a tree, but restrict each split. Grow as usual with one change: at every node, choose at random a subset of \(m\) inputs and consider splits on those only. A different subset is drawn at each node.
  3. Grow it deep and leave it unpruned. Individual overfitting is acceptable here; averaging will deal with it.
  4. Repeat B times. The trees are independent of one another, so this can be done in any order or all at once.
  5. Combine. For classification take the majority vote; for numeric outputs take the average.
  6. Score for free. Each observation was left out of about a third of the trees. Predict it using only those trees and you get an honest error estimate without holding any data back.
Worked by hand

Adding trees drives the second part of the variance to zero but cannot touch the first. Restricting the inputs at each split is what lowers the floor, by preventing one dominant input from appearing at the top of every tree.

Why the answer is correct

Averaging never increases variance and leaves bias unchanged. Since a deep tree has low bias and high variance, averaging is exactly the right correction.

Why it stops

\(B\) rounds, fixed in advance.

What it costs

\(B\) times the cost of one tree, but perfectly parallel.

Where it goes wrong
  • Adding more trees never hurts accuracy, so a forest cannot be overfitted by \(B\). It can be overfitted by growing trees too deep on noisy data, or by setting \(m\) too large.
  • With many irrelevant inputs, a small \(m\) means most splits see nothing useful. Raise it.

Gradient boosting

Ensemble \(\cdot\) sequential, attacks bias

Build a model in small stages, each stage fitted to what the previous stages still get wrong.

Input and output

Given. A labelled table, a small shrinkage factor \(\nu\), a tree depth (3 to 6), and a number of rounds.

Produce. A sum of many small trees.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Start with the simplest possible answer. Predict the same constant everywhere — the overall average for numeric outputs.
  2. Compute what is still wrong. For every observation, take the difference between the truth and the current prediction. For squared error these are just the leftovers; for other losses they are the direction in which the prediction should move.
  3. Fit a small tree to those leftovers. The tree is not predicting the outcome; it is predicting the current mistakes. Keep it shallow — it only needs to capture a little structure.
  4. Add a fraction of it. Add the new tree's predictions to the running total, multiplied by \(\nu\) (typically 0.01 to 0.1). Taking only a fraction of each step is what keeps the procedure stable.
  5. Repeat, and watch a held-out set. Recompute what is wrong and go again. Unlike a forest, this will eventually overfit, so stop when the held-out error stops improving.

Each round removes half the remaining error. With a smaller shrinkage the steps are smaller and more rounds are needed, but the final answer generalises better.

Why the answer is correct

Each stage moves the predictions in the direction that most reduces the loss — the same principle as gradient descent, carried out in the space of functions rather than of coefficients.

Why it stops

A fixed number of rounds, or early stopping on held-out error.

What it costs

One shallow tree per round, and the rounds cannot be parallelised because each depends on the last.

Where it goes wrong
  • Shrinkage and the number of rounds trade off directly. Halving the shrinkage roughly doubles the rounds needed.
  • Sensitive to mislabelled observations: a wrong label is a permanent large error that every subsequent round chases.
  • Many interacting settings. Tune the shrinkage and the number of rounds together, not separately.
