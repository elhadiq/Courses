Section 3 — Decision Trees and Ensemble Methods

Recursive partitioning, impurity criteria, and why averaging many weak, decorrelated models is the most reliable trick in classical machine learning.

3.1 Recursive partitioning

A decision tree carves the input space into axis-aligned boxes and fits a constant in each. Formally, a tree with leaves \(R_1\), …, \(R_{M}\) predicts:

\[f(x) = \sum_{m=1..M} c_{m} \cdot 1[ x \in R_{m} ]\]

For squared loss the optimal leaf value \(c_{m}\) is the mean of the targets in \(R_{m}\); for classification it is the majority class, and the leaf's class proportions give a probability estimate. What remains is choosing the boxes — and that is the hard part.

Why the algorithm is greedy
Hyafil and Rivest [4] proved that constructing the optimal binary decision tree is NP-complete. Every practical algorithm — CART [1], ID3 [2], C4.5 [3] — is therefore a greedy top-down heuristic: pick the locally best split, recurse, never reconsider. There is no optimality guarantee, and this is the root cause of a tree's instability.

Impurity criteria

A split is scored by how much it purifies the child nodes. Writing \(\hat{p}_{mk}\) for the proportion of class k in node m, the three classical impurities are:

\[\mathbf{Misclassification}: 1 - \max_{k} \hat{p}_{mk}\]
\[\mathbf{Gini} (\operatorname{CART}): \sum_{k} \hat{p}_{mk}(1 - \hat{p}_{mk}) = 1 - \sum_{k} \hat{p}_{mk}^2\]
\[\mathbf{Entropy} (ID3/C4.5): -\sum_{k} \hat{p}_{mk} \log_2 \hat{p}_{mk}\]

The chosen split maximises the impurity decrease, weighted by child sizes:

\[\Delta I = I(parent) - (n_{L}/n) I(left) - (n_{R}/n) I(right)\]

Gini and entropy are strictly concave, misclassification error is only piecewise linear. That difference matters: a split that moves 100 points from a 50/50 node into 75/25 and 25/75 children leaves misclassification error unchanged (\(\Delta I = 0\)) but strictly improves Gini and entropy. Concavity is what makes the greedy search able to see progress [1].

Pruning

Grown to purity a tree memorises the training set. CART's answer is cost-complexity pruning [1]: grow a large tree \(T_0\), then minimise over subtrees

\[C_{\alpha}(T) = \sum_{m} n_{m} I(R_{m}) + \alpha |T|\]

|T| is the number of leaves, so \(\alpha\) buys simplicity exactly as \(\lambda\) did in ridge regression. Breiman et al. proved that as \(\alpha\) increases the optimal subtrees form a nested sequence obtained by weakest-link pruning, so the whole regularisation path can be computed in one pass and \(\alpha\) selected by cross-validation.

That last row is the opening for everything that follows. A single tree is a low-bias, very high-variance learner. Section 1 told us that variance is reduced by averaging.

3.2 Bagging: variance reduction by averaging

Breiman's bagging [5] (bootstrap aggregating) fits B trees, each on a bootstrap resample of the training set (n points drawn with replacement), and averages their predictions.

Why averaging works — and where it stops
If B predictors each have variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\), the variance of their mean is
\[Var( \bar{f} ) = \rho \sigma^2 + (1 - \rho )\sigma^2 / B\]

The second term vanishes as \(B \to \infty\), but the first does not. The floor of an ensemble is set by the correlation between its members, not by their number. Adding trees past a few hundred buys nothing; decorrelating them does.

Bagging also gives free validation. Each bootstrap sample omits about (1 \(- 1/n)^{n} \to e^{-1} \approx\) 36.8 % of the data; averaging each point's prediction over only the trees that did not see it yields the out-of-bag error, an unbiased risk estimate at no extra cost [5].

3.3 Random forests

Random forests [7] add a second source of randomness on top of bagging, drawing on Ho's random subspace idea [6]: at every split, only a random subset of m features is considered.

\[m = \lfloor \sqrt{d}\rfloor \text{for classification } | m = \lfloor d/3\rfloor \text{for regression } (\text{defaults from } [7])\]

The effect is precisely the one the variance formula asks for. Without it, a single dominant feature is chosen at the root of nearly every tree and all trees look alike (\(\rho\) high). Forcing most trees to ignore it at any given split makes them structurally different, lowering \(\rho\) at a small cost in individual accuracy — a trade that the formula shows is almost always profitable.

Theorem — Random forests do not overfit with B [7]
Breiman proved that as \(B \to \infty\) the generalisation error converges almost surely to a limit bounded by \(\bar{\rho}(1 - s^2)/s^2\), where s is the average margin (strength) of the individual trees and \(\bar{\rho}\) their mean correlation. Adding trees never hurts; it only fails to help past a point. Depth and m are the parameters that can overfit.

Two forms of variable importance come out of the fitting process:

  • Mean decrease in impurity (MDI) — total \(\Delta I\) attributable to each feature, summed over all splits. Free, but Strobl et al. [12] showed it is biased toward high-cardinality and continuous features, which simply offer more candidate split points.
  • Permutation importance — the drop in out-of-bag accuracy when a feature's values are shuffled. Model-agnostic and far more trustworthy, though it misattributes among correlated features.
  • SHAP values [13] — the unique additive attribution satisfying the Shapley axioms, with an exact polynomial-time algorithm for trees. Now the standard for per-prediction explanation.

3.4 Boosting: fitting the residual

Bagging builds trees in parallel from independent resamples. Boosting builds them sequentially, each one correcting its predecessors. The two attack different terms of the decomposition: bagging reduces variance, boosting reduces bias.

AdaBoost

Freund and Schapire [8] reweight the training points at each round, up-weighting those the current ensemble gets wrong, and give each weak learner a vote proportional to its accuracy:

\[\alpha_{t} = \tfrac{1}{2} \ln[ (1 - \epsilon_{t}) / \epsilon_{t} ], w_{i} \leftarrow w_{i} \exp(-\alpha_{t} y_{i} h_{t}(x_{i}))\]

Their theorem: if every weak learner beats chance by \(\gamma \gt 0\), the training error drops exponentially, below exp(\(-2T\gamma^2\)) after T rounds. It was later recognised that AdaBoost is exactly forward stagewise additive modelling under the exponential loss \(e^{-yf(x)}\) — another member of the surrogate-loss family from Section 2.5, and the reason AdaBoost is sensitive to label noise (the exponential loss punishes a badly misclassified point without limit).

Gradient boosting

Friedman [9] generalised this to any differentiable loss by reading boosting as gradient descent in function space. At stage t, compute the negative gradient of the loss at the current predictions — the pseudo-residuals — and fit the next tree to them:

\[r_{i}^{(t)} = - \partial L(y_{i}, F(x_{i})) / \partial F(x_{i}) \text{evaluated at } F = F^{(t-1)}\]
\[F^{(t)}(x) = F^{(t-1)}(x) + \nu \cdot h_{t}(x), 0 \lt \nu \le 1\]

For squared loss the pseudo-residuals are the ordinary residuals, which makes the idea transparent: every tree fits what the previous ones got wrong. The shrinkage rate \(\nu (\)typically 0.01–0.1) trades against the number of trees — small \(\nu\) needs more rounds but generalises better.

def gradient_boost_squared(X, y, n_trees=100, nu=0.1, fit_tree=None):
    """Skeleton of gradient boosting under squared loss.
    fit_tree(X, r) must return a callable predicting the pseudo-residuals r."""
    F0 = sum(y) / len(y)                 # the constant that minimises squared loss
    F = [F0] * len(y)
    trees = []
    for _ in range(n_trees):
        residuals = [yi - Fi for yi, Fi in zip(y, F)]   # = negative gradient / 2
        h = fit_tree(X, residuals)
        preds = [h(xi) for xi in X]
        F = [Fi + nu * pi for Fi, pi in zip(F, preds)]
        trees.append(h)
    return F0, trees, nu

XGBoost [10] sharpened this in three ways that made it dominate tabular competitions: a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss (using both gradient and Hessian) rather than first-order; an explicit \(\gamma |T| + \tfrac{1}{2} \lambda \| w\|^2\) regularisation term inside the split criterion; and systems work — sparsity-aware splitting, cache-aware column blocks, approximate quantile sketching. LightGBM [11] added histogram binning and leaf-wise growth for a further order of magnitude on large data.

3.5 Choosing between them

Random ForestGradient Boosting
Attacksvariancebias
Trees aredeep, independent, parallelshallow (depth 3–6), sequential
Overfits with more treesno [7]yes — needs early stopping
Tuning burdenlow (m, depth)high (\(\nu\), depth, subsample, \(\lambda , \gamma\))
Parallelismtrivial across treesonly within a tree
Noise robustnessgoodsensitive, especially AdaBoost
Typical accuracy on tabular datastrong baselineusually the winner when tuned

A pragmatic rule: fit a random forest first because it works out of the box and its out-of-bag error is free; move to gradient boosting when the last few points of accuracy justify the tuning budget.

Summary

  • Optimal trees are NP-complete [4]; all real algorithms are greedy, which is why single trees are unstable.
  • Gini and entropy are concave, misclassification error is not — concavity is what makes greedy splitting see progress [1].
  • Var(\(\bar{f}) = \rho \sigma^2 + (1-\rho )\sigma^2/B\): ensemble quality is capped by member correlation [5].
  • Random forests decorrelate by sampling features at each split; OOB error is free validation [7].
  • Boosting is gradient descent in function space, fitting each tree to the pseudo-residuals [9]; XGBoost adds second-order information and explicit regularisation [10].
  • Prefer permutation importance or SHAP [13] over impurity importance, which is biased [12].
