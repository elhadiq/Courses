Section 6 — Graph Cuts and Spectral Clustering

From the NP-hard balanced-cut problem to an eigenvector computation: the relaxation that lets linear algebra find communities k-means cannot see.

6.1 Clustering as a cut problem

Section 4 ended with a failure: k-means cannot separate two interleaved crescents or two concentric rings, because its objective assumes clusters are Euclidean balls. Spectral clustering replaces that assumption with a different one — that clusters are well-connected regions of a similarity graph — and the change of assumption is the entire content of the method.

First build a graph from the data if you do not already have one. Three standard choices [6]:

  • \(\epsilon -\)neighbourhood: join points closer than \(\epsilon\). Unweighted, and fragile when densities vary.
  • k-nearest-neighbour: join u to its k nearest neighbours (symmetrised by OR, or by AND for the "mutual" variant). Robust to varying density — the usual default.
  • Fully connected with a Gaussian kernel: \(w_{uv} =exp(-\| x_{u} - x_{v}\|^2 / 2\sigma^2\)). Dense, so O(\(n^2\)), but smooth.
\(\sigma\) and k are the real hyperparameters
Spectral clustering is highly sensitive to the similarity graph, far more than to anything downstream. \(A \sigma\) too large connects everything and the graph has one community; too small and it shatters into singletons. Zelnik-Manor and Perona [11] proposed a local scale \(\sigma_{u} =\) distance to u's 7th nearest neighbour, giving \(w_{uv} =exp(-d^2/\sigma_{u}\sigma_{v}\)) — a cheap fix that handles clusters of different densities and should generally be preferred to a global \(\sigma\).

Which cut?

Definition — Cut, RatioCut, NCut
For a partition into A and its complement \(\overline{A}\), write cut(A, \(\overline{A}) = \sum_{u\in A, v\in \overline{A}} w_{uv}\). Then
\[RatioCut(A) = cut(A,\overline{A})/|A| + cut(A,\overline{A})/|\overline{A}| NCut(A) = cut(A,\overline{A})/vol(A) + cut(A,\overline{A})/vol(\overline{A})\]

where vol(A) = \(\sum_{u\in A}d_{u}\). RatioCut balances by number of nodes, NCut by total degree.

The plain minimum cut is useless for clustering: it is solvable in polynomial time, but its optimum almost always isolates a single low-degree node, since that costs only one edge. The denominators in RatioCut and NCut are what force balance — and they are also what makes the problem NP-hard [8]. As with trees (Section 3) and k-means (Section 4), we relax rather than solve.

6.2 The relaxation

Take k = 2 and encode the partition in a single vector. Following Hagen and Kahng [3], define

\[f_{u} = \sqrt{|\overline{A}|/|A|} if u \in A, -\sqrt{|A|/|\overline{A}|} if u \in \overline{A}\]

Three short computations (all in [6]) show that this f satisfies:

\[f^{T}Lf = |V| \cdot RatioCut(A), f \perp \mathbf{1}, \| f\|^2 = |V|\]

So minimising RatioCut is exactly equivalent to

\[\min_{f} f^{T}Lf \text{subject to } f \perp \mathbf{1}, \| f\| = \sqrt{n}, f \text{taking only those two values }\]

The last constraint — discreteness — is the NP-hard part. Drop it and allow \(f \in\) R\(^{n}\). What remains is a Rayleigh-quotient minimisation over the subspace orthogonal to the constant vector, and the Courant–Fischer theorem answers it immediately.

Theorem — Rayleigh–Ritz / Courant–Fischer
For a symmetric L with eigenvalues 0 = \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le\) … and eigenvectors \(u_1 =\) 1/\(\sqrt{n}, u_2\), …,
\[\min_{f \perp u_1, f \ne 0} f^{T}Lf / f^{T}f = \lambda_2, \text{attained at } f = u_2\]

The solution of the relaxed problem is therefore the Fiedler vector [2]. Recovering a partition from it requires a rounding step: take the sign of each entry, or the median, or — best — sweep over all thresholds and keep the one with the lowest cut.

Because we relaxed, \(\lambda_2/2 \cdot n\) is only a lower bound on the true RatioCut [1]. How loose can the bound be? Cheeger's inequality [9] in its graph form [10] gives the answer, and it is a strong one.

Theorem — Cheeger's inequality (graph form)
Let \(\phi (G) =\min_{A}cut(A,\overline{A})/\)min(vol A, vol \(\overline{A}\)) be the conductance of G, and \(\lambda_2\) the second eigenvalue of \(L_{sym}\). Then
\[\lambda_2 / 2 \le \phi (G) \le \sqrt{2\lambda_2}\]

The relaxation is provably within a quadratic factor of optimal, and the sweep cut over the Fiedler vector achieves the upper bound. This is why a method that ignores an NP-hard constraint nevertheless works: the spectral gap and the best cut control each other. Lee, Oveis Gharan and Trevisan [12] extended this to k-way partitions using the first k eigenvectors.

6.3 The algorithms

Three variants are in common use. They differ only in which Laplacian is used and whether rows are renormalised [6].

AlgorithmMatrixEmbedding rowsRelaxes
Unnormalised [3]L = D − Afirst k eigenvectors as columnsRatioCut
Shi–Malik [4]\(L_{rw} = D^{-1}L\)generalised eigenvectors Lu = \(\lambda\)DuNCut
Ng–Jordan–Weiss [5]\(L_{sym} = D^{-1/2}LD^{-1/2}\)rows normalised to unit lengthNCut
The algorithm in five lines
1. Build the similarity graph and its weight matrix W. 2. Form the chosen Laplacian. 3. Compute the eigenvectors \(u_1\), …, \(u_{k}\) of the k smallest eigenvalues. 4. Stack them as columns of \(U \in\) R\(^{n\times k}\); row i is the new representation of node i (normalise rows for NJW). 5. Run k-means on those n rows.

Step 5 deserves comment. It looks circular — we invoked spectral methods precisely because k-means failed — but the failure was one of geometry, not of the algorithm. The spectral embedding is a change of coordinates in which the crescents become well-separated blobs, and in those coordinates k-means is exactly the right tool. Spectral clustering is k-means applied after a non-linear, connectivity-driven re-embedding.

The embedding itself has independent value: it is precisely the Laplacian eigenmap of Belkin and Niyogi [7], the manifold-learning method promised at the end of Section 4. Minimising \(\sum_{uv}w_{uv}\| y_{u} - y_{v}\|^2\) subject to an orthogonality constraint is the same eigenproblem — embedding for visualisation and clustering by cuts are, algebraically, one procedure.

import numpy as np

def spectral_clustering(W, k):
    """Ng-Jordan-Weiss spectral clustering on a similarity matrix W (n x n, symmetric)."""
    d = W.sum(axis=1)
    d_inv_sqrt = 1.0 / np.sqrt(np.maximum(d, 1e-12))
    L_sym = np.eye(len(d)) - (d_inv_sqrt[:, None] * W * d_inv_sqrt[None, :])

    vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(L_sym)          # ascending, orthonormal
    U = vecs[:, :k]                             # k smallest eigenvalues

    norms = np.linalg.norm(U, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    U = U / np.maximum(norms, 1e-12)            # NJW row normalisation

    return kmeans(U, k), vals                   # any k-means from Section 4

Choosing k: the eigengap heuristic

If the graph had exactly k connected components, L would have eigenvalue 0 with multiplicity k and \(\lambda_{k+1} \gt 0 (\)Section 5.2). Real graphs are perturbations of that ideal, so by matrix perturbation theory the first k eigenvalues stay near zero and a visible gap opens before \(\lambda_{k+1}\). Choose k at the largest gap [6].

As bridging edges are added the zeros lift off but stay small, and the gap — though shrinking — still identifies k = 3. Push far enough and the gap disappears entirely: the heuristic is honest about telling you there is no clear community structure left to find.

6.4 Practical limits

IssueConsequenceMitigation
Full eigendecomposition is O(\(n^3\))infeasible past ~\(10^4\) nodesLanczos / ARPACK on the sparse matrix (only k eigenvectors needed), or Nyström sub-sampling
Transductive by constructiona new point requires recomputing the eigenvectorsout-of-sample extension via the Nyström formula; or move to a GNN (Section 8)
Very sensitive to \(\sigma\) and k of the graphunstable clusteringslocal scaling [11]; inspect the graph before trusting the output
Assumes balanced clustersNCut's denominator penalises small clustersuse k larger than expected and merge, or switch to a density method
Rounding is heuristicthe relaxed solution is not a partitionsweep cut with the Cheeger guarantee [9] rather than a plain sign cut

Even with these caveats spectral clustering remains a first-choice method whenever the data come as a graph, or whenever cluster shape is unknown and n is moderate. Shi and Malik's normalized cuts [4] was the state of the art in image segmentation for a decade for exactly this reason, and its NCut objective is still a standard baseline.

Summary

  • Balanced graph cuts (RatioCut, NCut) are NP-hard [8]; spectral clustering drops the discreteness constraint.
  • The relaxed problem is min \(f^{T}\)Lf over \(f \perp\) 1, solved by the Fiedler vector [2].
  • Cheeger's inequality \(\lambda_2/2 \le \phi (G) \le \sqrt{2\lambda_2}\) bounds how much the relaxation can lose [9] [10].
  • RatioCut \(\to L, NCut\to L_{rw}\) [4] or \(L_{sym}\) [5].
  • The k eigenvectors give an embedding — identical to Laplacian eigenmaps [7] — in which k-means finally works.
  • Choose k by the eigengap; choose the similarity graph very carefully [6].
  • O(\(n^3\)) cost and transductivity are the two limits that motivate the scalable, inductive methods of Sections 7 and 8.
