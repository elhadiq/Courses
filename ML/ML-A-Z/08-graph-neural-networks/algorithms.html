One round of message passing

Layered method \(\cdot\) one hop per layer

Give every node a description that reflects both its own attributes and those of the nodes around it.

Input and output

Given. A graph, a set of attributes per node, and a number of rounds \(L\).

Produce. A new description of every node.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Add a self-loop to every node. Pretend each node is joined to itself. Without this a node would describe its surroundings while forgetting itself entirely.
  2. Gather from the neighbours. For each node, collect the current descriptions of the nodes joined to it. Combine them into one, using an operation that does not depend on the order — adding, averaging or taking the largest. Order-independence is essential, because the nodes have no natural numbering.
  3. Weight by connectedness. Divide each contribution by the square root of the product of the two nodes' connection counts. Without this, heavily connected nodes overwhelm everything, and repeated rounds cause the numbers to grow without bound.
  4. Mix and reshape. Apply the same linear transformation at every node — the part that is learned — and then a simple non-linear function.
  5. Repeat L times. After \(L\) rounds, each node's description reflects everything within \(L\) hops of it. Two or three rounds is usual; the diagnosis below explains why more is rarely better.

Information reaches exactly one hop further per round. But look at the later rounds: the values are converging to the same number everywhere. That is over-smoothing, visible in five nodes.

Why the answer is correct

Relabelling the nodes relabels the output identically and changes nothing else. This is the property that makes the method well defined on a graph, where there is no first node.

Why it stops

\(L\) rounds, fixed in advance.

What it costs

Proportional to the number of edges times the description size, per round.

Where it goes wrong
  • It assumes joined nodes are alike. Where edges tend to join unlike nodes, this averaging destroys exactly the signal you need, and a method ignoring the graph does better.
  • Omitting the self-loop, or the degree weighting, produces a method that either forgets the node or blows up numerically after a few rounds.

Neighbour sampling for large graphs

Sampling \(\cdot\) makes the cost predictable

Apply the same idea to a graph too large to process whole, and to nodes that did not exist at training time.

Input and output

Given. A large graph and a budget of neighbours per round, for instance 25 then 10.

Produce. Descriptions for any requested nodes, computed without touching the whole graph.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Start from the nodes you actually need. Only those whose descriptions are required, not every node in the graph.
  2. Sample their neighbours. For each, choose at random a fixed number of neighbours rather than using all of them. A node with a million connections costs the same as one with twenty.
  3. Sample again, one level out. Repeat for the neighbours just chosen, with a smaller budget. The result is a small tree around each target node.
  4. Combine inwards. Work from the outside in, combining descriptions level by level until reaching the target.
  5. Keep the node separate from its neighbourhood. Rather than averaging the node in with its neighbours, hold the two apart and combine them explicitly. This preserves the distinction between what a node is and what surrounds it.
Why the answer is correct

What is learned is a rule for combining a neighbourhood, not a separate description for each node. The same rule therefore applies to a node never seen before — or to an entirely new graph.

Why it stops

Fixed number of rounds and a fixed budget per round.

What it costs

The product of the per-round budgets, per target node — independent of the size of the graph. This is what makes the method deployable.

Where it goes wrong
  • Sampling adds noise: two runs give slightly different answers. Average over several samples at prediction time if stability matters.
  • Budgets that grow with depth cause the cost to explode. Reduce them at each level.

Colour refinement and the expressiveness limit

Test \(\cdot\) establishes what is impossible

Determine whether two graphs can be told apart by any method that works by passing messages between neighbours.

Input and output

Given. Two graphs.

Produce. Either a proof that they differ, or the answer 'cannot tell'.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Give every node the same colour. Start with no distinctions at all.
  2. Describe each node by itself and its neighbours. Form, for each node, the pair consisting of its own colour and the collection of its neighbours' colours. The collection is unordered but keeps repetitions — two neighbours coloured red is different from one.
  3. Recolour. Give the same new colour to nodes with identical descriptions, and different colours to nodes with different ones.
  4. Repeat until nothing changes. The division of nodes into colours can only get finer, so this settles.
  5. Compare. Tally how many nodes have each colour in each graph. Different tallies prove the graphs differ. Identical tallies prove nothing.

The graphs are plainly different — one has triangles, the other does not — yet this procedure cannot see it. No message-passing method can either, at any depth or width. That is a hard ceiling, not a shortcoming of any particular design.

Why the answer is correct

Every message-passing method is at most as discerning as this test, and reaches that ceiling only when its combining operation never conflates two different collections. Averaging conflates one item with two identical items; taking the largest conflates repetitions; adding does neither. This is the whole argument for summing.

Why it stops

Each round refines the division or leaves it unchanged; there are at most as many rounds as nodes.

What it costs

\(O(m)\) per round.

Where it goes wrong
  • Because of this ceiling, such methods cannot count triangles or detect cycle lengths — properties that decide how a molecule behaves.
  • The way round is to give nodes extra distinguishing information: counts of local structures, distances, or positions derived from the graph's own spectrum.

Diagnosing over-smoothing

Diagnostic \(\cdot\) decides how deep to go

Measure whether adding rounds is still helping or has begun to destroy the distinctions you need.

Input and output

Given. A graph and the node descriptions after each round.

Produce. A curve that says how many rounds are useful on this graph.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Measure how much neighbours differ. After each round, go over the edges and add up the squared differences between the descriptions at the two ends, each divided by the square root of its node's connection count.
  2. Divide by the overall size. So that the number reflects the shape of the descriptions and not their scale.
  3. Plot it against the number of rounds, on a logarithmic vertical scale. A straight downward line means the quantity is being multiplied by the same factor every round.
  4. Read the rate off the slope. That factor is governed by how well connected the graph is. A graph with a narrow waist has a factor close to one and degrades slowly; a well-connected graph collapses within a handful of rounds.
  5. Choose the depth. Deep enough to reach the information you need, shallow enough that the measure has not collapsed. In practice this is almost always two or three.

Both collapse. Only the timescale differs, and it is set by the same property of the graph that decides how easy it is to cut in two — the connection to Section 6 is exact, not an analogy.

Why the answer is correct

Repeated averaging drives every description towards the same direction, which carries no information distinguishing one node from another. The measure is exactly the disagreement across edges of Section 5, so it reaching zero and the descriptions becoming identical are the same statement.

Why it stops

Direct.

What it costs

\(O(m)\) per round measured.

Where it goes wrong
  • The opposite failure also exists: too few rounds and information from distant but relevant nodes never arrives, having been squeezed through a narrow waist into a fixed-size description. The two failures pull in opposite directions and on some graphs no depth avoids both.
  • Skip connections, keeping a share of the original description at every round, and adding edges around the narrow waist are the standard remedies.
