Section 7 — Random Walks, PageRank and Node Embeddings

Centrality by random surfing, shallow embeddings learned from walk co-occurrence, and the link-prediction task that made them useful.

7.1 The random walk as a probe

A random walk on a graph is the simplest process that turns structure into numbers. From node u, step to a uniformly chosen neighbour. The state distribution evolves by

\[\pi^{(t+1)} = P^{T}\pi^{(t)}, P = D^{-1}A\]

P is row-stochastic, and note that P = \(I - L_{rw}\) — the random-walk Laplacian of Section 5 is literally "one minus one step of the walk". Everything spectral we proved there transfers here.

Theorem — Stationary distribution of an undirected walk
If G is connected, undirected and non-bipartite, the walk has a unique stationary distribution and it is proportional to degree:
\[\pi_{u} = d_{u} / 2m\]

Proof. (\(P^{T}\pi )_{v} = \sum_{u}(A_{uv}/d_{u})(d_{u}/2m) = (\sum_{u}A_{uv})/2m = d_{v}/2m\). Uniqueness follows from Perron–Frobenius [4]. \(\square\)

The consequence is a warning: on an undirected graph, "how often does a long walk visit u" is just the degree and tells you nothing new. The interesting quantities are all about short, restarted, or directed walks.

7.2 PageRank

On the directed Web graph the naïve walk breaks in two ways: dangling nodes with no out-links absorb all the probability, and spider traps — groups of pages linking only to each other — accumulate rank without ever releasing it. Page, Brin, Motwani and Winograd [2] [1] fixed both with a single device: at each step, with probability \(1 - \alpha\), teleport to a uniformly random page.

\[\mathbf{r = \alpha P^{T} r + (1 - \alpha ) (1/n) 1}, \alpha \approx 0.85\]
Why PageRank is well defined
The teleport term makes the modified transition matrix \(\tilde{G} = \alpha P + (1-\alpha )(1/n)11^{T}\) strictly positive, hence irreducible and aperiodic. By Perron–Frobenius [4] it has a unique positive dominant eigenvector with eigenvalue 1, and power iteration converges to it geometrically at rate \(\alpha\). With \(\alpha = 0.85\), each iteration removes about 15 % of the error, so 50–100 iterations suffice for any graph size — and each iteration costs only O(m), because P is sparse and the rank-one teleport term is handled as a scalar.

\(\alpha\) is a genuine trade-off: larger \(\alpha\) respects the link structure more but converges more slowly and is more vulnerable to link spam.

def pagerank(out_links, alpha=0.85, tol=1e-10, max_iter=100):
    """Power iteration. out_links maps node -> list of successors. O(m) per iteration."""
    nodes = list(out_links)
    n = len(nodes)
    r = {u: 1.0 / n for u in nodes}
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        new = {u: 0.0 for u in nodes}
        dangling = 0.0
        for u in nodes:
            succ = out_links[u]
            if not succ:                       # dangling: redistribute uniformly
                dangling += r[u]
                continue
            share = r[u] / len(succ)
            for v in succ:
                new[v] += share
        base = (1.0 - alpha) / n + alpha * dangling / n
        new = {u: base + alpha * new[u] for u in nodes}
        if sum(abs(new[u] - r[u]) for u in nodes) < tol:
            return new
        r = new
    return r

Convergence is visibly geometric, and the final ranking is not the in-degree ranking — a single link from a high-rank page outweighs many links from obscure ones, which is the whole point of the recursion.

Two variants matter in practice. Personalised PageRank [5] replaces the uniform teleport vector by one concentrated on a seed set S; the resulting scores measure proximity to S and are the standard graph-based recommendation and local-community-detection primitive. HITS [3] instead computes two mutually reinforcing scores — hubs point to good authorities, authorities are pointed to by good hubs — which are the dominant eigenvectors of \(A^{T}A\) and \(AA^{T}\).

7.3 Shallow node embeddings

PageRank gives each node one number. Modern pipelines want a vector \(z_{u} \in\) R\(^{d}\) per node, so that any downstream classifier from Part I can be applied. The encoder–decoder framing [14] makes the design space clear:

\[\operatorname{ENC}: u \mapsto z_{u} | \operatorname{DEC}(z_{u}, z_{v}) \approx sim_{G}(u, v)\]

A shallow encoder is just a lookup table \(Z \in\) R\(^{n\times d}\) — one free vector per node, trained directly. The decoder is usually an inner product or a sigmoid of one. What distinguishes the methods is entirely the definition of \(sim_{G}\).

DeepWalk

Perozzi, Al-Rfou and Skiena [7] made the analogy that started the field: a random walk on a graph is a sentence, and a node is a word. Generate many short walks, feed them to word2vec's skip-gram model [6], and read off the learned vectors.

\[\max_{Z} \sum_{u\in V} \sum_{v\in Nwalk}(u) \log P(v | z_{u}), P(v|z_{u}) = \exp(z_{u}^{T}z_{v}) / \sum_{w} \exp(z_{u}^{T}z_{w})\]

The softmax denominator costs O(n) per term, which is fatal at web scale. Two standard approximations: hierarchical softmax (a binary tree over nodes, O(log n)) used in the original paper, and negative sampling — contrast each true pair against a few random ones — which is simpler and now more common.

LINE and node2vec

LINE [8] dispenses with walks and optimises first-order proximity (observed edges) and second-order proximity (shared neighbourhoods) explicitly, concatenating the two embeddings.

node2vec [9] keeps DeepWalk's architecture and changes only the walk. It is a second-order walk: the probability of the next step depends on where you came from. Having just moved \(t \to v\), the unnormalised weight of moving to x is

\[\alpha (t, x) = 1/p if x = t \cdot \cdot \cdot 1 if d(t,x) = 1 \cdot \cdot \cdot 1/q if d(t,x) = 2\]
ParameterEffectSearch behaviourWhat the embedding captures
p largediscourages immediate backtracking—less redundancy
q \(\gt 1\)down-weights moving awayBFS-like, stays localstructural equivalence — nodes with similar roles (both hubs, both bridges) get similar vectors even if far apart
q \(\lt 1\)encourages moving outwardDFS-like, explores farhomophily — nodes in the same community get similar vectors

This is the practically important knob, because the two notions of similarity are genuinely different and different tasks want different ones. Grover and Leskovec tuned (p, q) by cross-validation and showed the gain over fixed-walk DeepWalk is substantial.

They are all matrix factorisation

Theorem — Qiu et al. (2018) [10]
DeepWalk, LINE, PTE and node2vec with negative sampling all implicitly factorise a closed-form matrix built from powers of P = \(D^{-1}A\). For DeepWalk with window T and b negative samples, the implicit target is
\[\log( (vol(G)/(bT)) \sum_{r=1..T} P^{r} D^{-1} )\]

Their NetMF algorithm factorises this matrix directly and matches or beats the sampling-based originals. The lesson generalises: these "neural" embeddings are spectral methods in disguise, close relatives of the Laplacian eigenmaps of Section 6.

The three structural limits of shallow embeddings
(1) No parameter sharing — O(nd) free parameters, one block per node, so nothing is learned that transfers between nodes. (2) Strictly transductive — a new node has no row in Z, so it has no embedding, and the whole model must be retrained. (3) Node features are ignored — the encoder sees only the adjacency structure, discarding X entirely. All three are fixed by replacing the lookup table with a function of the neighbourhood. That function is a graph neural network, and it is Section 8.

7.4 Link prediction

Given a snapshot of a graph, which edges are missing or will appear next? Liben-Nowell and Kleinberg [11] framed the problem and benchmarked the classical similarity scores, which remain surprisingly strong baselines.

ScoreDefinitionIdea
Common neighbours|\(\Gamma (u) \cap \Gamma (v)|\)triadic closure
Jaccard|\(\Gamma (u) \cap \Gamma (v)| / |\Gamma (u) \cup \Gamma (v)|\)normalised for degree
Adamic–Adar [12]\(\sum_{z\in \Gamma (u)\cap \Gamma (v)} 1 /logd_{z}\)a shared rare neighbour is far more informative than a shared hub
Preferential attachment\(d_{u} \cdot d_{v}\)rich get richer [11]
Katz\(\sum_{l\ge 1} \beta^{l} |paths^{(l)}(u,v)|\)all paths, damped by length — a walk-count, cf. Section 5.2
Embedding dot product\(z_{u}^{T}z_{v}\)learned similarity [9]
Evaluating link prediction correctly
Held-out edges must be deleted from the graph before any score is computed, otherwise every method reads the answer off the adjacency matrix. Negatives must be sampled deliberately: with \(n^2\) possible pairs and only \(m \approx n\) real edges, uniformly sampled negatives are trivially easy and accuracy near 100 % means nothing. Report AUC or Hits@k against hard negatives — typically non-edges at distance 2 — and be explicit about the sampling scheme.

For knowledge graphs, where edges are typed triples (head, relation, tail), the decoder must involve the relation. TransE [13] models it as translation, scoring a triple by \(\| z_{h} + r - z_{t}\|\) and training with a margin ranking loss — simple, scalable, and still a standard baseline despite known limits on symmetric and one-to-many relations.

Summary

  • P = \(D^{-1}A = I - L_{rw}\); on undirected graphs the stationary distribution is just degree, so only short, restarted or directed walks are informative.
  • PageRank [2] adds teleportation, which guarantees a unique solution by Perron–Frobenius and geometric convergence at rate \(\alpha\).
  • DeepWalk [7] = random walks + skip-gram; node2vec [9] biases the walk with (p, q) to interpolate between structural equivalence and homophily.
  • All of these implicitly factorise a matrix of walk probabilities [10] — they are spectral methods in neural clothing.
  • Shallow embeddings are transductive, share no parameters and ignore node features — the three failures that motivate GNNs.
  • Adamic–Adar [12] and its relatives are strong, training-free link-prediction baselines; evaluate them with deleted edges and hard negatives [11].
