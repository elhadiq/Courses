Section 4 — Unsupervised Learning: Clustering and Dimensionality Reduction

4.1 Learning without labels

Unsupervised learning has no target y and therefore no risk to minimise in the sense of Section 1. The objective is instead structural: find a compressed description of the data that loses as little as possible. Two families dominate.

  • Clustering — compress the rows: replace each point by a group label.
  • Dimensionality reduction — compress the columns: replace each point by a shorter vector.
There is no ground truth to appeal to
Because no label exists, no clustering can be declared correct in the way a classifier can. Evaluation is either internal (silhouette [5], within-cluster inertia), external (compare against labels you happen to have but did not use), or extrinsic (does the clustering help a downstream task?). Internal indices measure whether the data match the algorithm's own assumptions — they cannot tell you the assumptions were right.

4.2 k-means and Lloyd's algorithm

k-means seeks k centroids \(\mu_1\), …, \(\mu_{k}\) and an assignment minimising the total within-cluster sum of squares (inertia):

\[J(C, \mu ) = \sum_{j=1..k} \sum_{x\in Cj} \| x - \mu_{j} \|^2_2\]

Aloise et al. [3] proved this is NP-hard even for k = 2 in the plane, so again (as with trees) we settle for a heuristic. Lloyd's algorithm [1] alternates two steps, each of which is optimal given the other:

  1. Assign — each point to its nearest centroid. (Optimal C for fixed \(\mu .\))
  2. Update — each centroid to the mean of its cluster. (Optimal \(\mu\) for fixed C, since the mean minimises the sum of squared distances.)
Convergence
Each step weakly decreases J, and there are finitely many partitions, so Lloyd's algorithm terminates in a finite number of iterations. It converges to a local minimum only, and the local minimum reached depends entirely on the initialisation — which is why the algorithm is restarted many times.

k-means++

Uniform random initialisation frequently places two centroids inside the same true cluster, which Lloyd's algorithm cannot repair. Arthur and Vassilvitskii [4] proposed seeding proportional to squared distance from the already-chosen centres:

import random

def kmeans_pp_init(points, k, seed=0):
    """D^2 seeding: the further a point is from existing centres, the likelier it is chosen."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    centres = [list(points[rng.randrange(len(points))])]
    for _ in range(k - 1):
        d2 = [min(sum((p[i] - c[i]) ** 2 for i in range(len(p))) for c in centres)
              for p in points]
        total = sum(d2)
        r, acc = rng.random() * total, 0.0
        for p, w in zip(points, d2):
            acc += w
            if acc >= r:
                centres.append(list(p))
                break
    return centres
Theorem — k-means++ guarantee [4]
With \(D^2\) seeding, the expected inertia satisfies E[J] \(\le 8(ln k + 2)J_{\operatorname{OPT}}\). This is an O(log k) approximation obtained before a single Lloyd iteration is run — and in practice it also makes the subsequent iterations converge faster. It is the default initialisation in every serious implementation.

Choosing k

Inertia decreases monotonically with k (k = n gives J = 0), so it cannot be minimised to select k. Two standard diagnostics:

  • The elbow — plot J against k and look for the kink where the marginal gain flattens. Informal and often ambiguous.
  • Silhouette [5] — for each point, s = (b − a)/max(a, b), where a is the mean distance to its own cluster and b the mean distance to the nearest other cluster. \(s \in [-1, 1]\); the average over all points is maximised at a plausible k. Negative s flags points assigned to the wrong cluster.

The assumptions hidden in the objective

k-means can only find spherical, comparably sized, linearly separable blobs
Minimising Euclidean sum of squares implicitly assumes isotropic clusters of similar radius and comparable population. It fails — predictably and badly — on elongated clusters, on nested rings, on two crescents, and whenever one cluster has far more points than another (the objective will split the large one and merge the small ones). This failure is the direct motivation for spectral clustering in Section 6, which replaces Euclidean geometry with connectivity on a graph.

Two escapes are available before we get to graphs. Gaussian mixture models fitted by EM replace hard assignment with posterior responsibilities and allow each cluster a full covariance matrix, so ellipsoids become expressible. DBSCAN [6] abandons centroids entirely, defining clusters as maximal sets of density-connected points; it discovers arbitrary shapes, infers the number of clusters itself, and labels low-density points as noise — at the cost of two parameters (\(\epsilon\), minPts) that are hard to set in high dimension.

4.3 Principal Component Analysis

PCA, introduced by Pearson [7] as the line of closest fit and recast in its modern algebraic form by Hotelling [8], finds the orthogonal directions along which centred data vary most.

\[w_1 = \operatorname{argmax}_{\| w\| =1} Var(Xw) = \operatorname{argmax}_{\| w\| =1} w^{T}\Sigma w, \Sigma = (1/n) X^{T}X\]
Theorem — PCA is an eigenproblem
By the Rayleigh quotient, the maximiser of \(w^{T}\Sigma w\) subject to ‖w‖ = 1 is the eigenvector of \(\Sigma\) with the largest eigenvalue, and the maximum value is that eigenvalue. Successive components are the remaining eigenvectors in decreasing order of \(\lambda\). Since \(\Sigma\) is real symmetric positive semi-definite, the spectral theorem guarantees an orthonormal eigenbasis with \(\lambda_{i} \ge 0\), and \(\lambda_{i}\) is exactly the variance captured by component i. This eigen-decomposition-of-a-symmetric-matrix pattern returns verbatim in Section 6 with the graph Laplacian.

Two equivalent readings, which is what makes PCA so central:

ReadingStatement
Maximum variancethe first p components span the p-dimensional subspace of greatest projected variance
Minimum reconstruction errorthat same subspace minimises \(\sum_{i}\| x_{i} - \hat{x}_{i}\|^2\) among all p-dimensional linear approximations — the Eckart–Young theorem [9].

In practice PCA is computed by the singular value decomposition X = \(U\Sigma V^{T}\) rather than by forming \(X^{T}X\), whose condition number is the square of X's. The columns of V are the components and \(\lambda_{i} = s_{i}^2/n\).

The dashed red line is the true first principal component — the orientation at which the projected variance is maximal, and equivalently at which the sum of squared perpendicular distances (Pearson's original formulation) is minimal.

Scaling is not optional
\(\Sigma\) is not invariant under rescaling of the features. A variable measured in metres and one measured in millimetres contribute variances differing by a factor of \(10^6\), and PCA will simply return the second one. Standardise every feature to zero mean and unit variance first, unless the features are already in comparable units [10]. Centring, by contrast, is mandatory: uncentred "PCA" returns the mean vector as its first component.

4.4 When linear is not enough: from manifolds to graphs

PCA can only rotate. If the data lie on a curved low-dimensional manifold — the "Swiss roll" is the canonical example — no linear projection unrolls it, and PCA will report a spuriously high dimension. The methods that solve this all share one move, and it is the move that Part II of this course is built on:

The key idea — replace global geometry by a local neighbourhood graph
Build a graph whose vertices are the data points and whose edges join each point to its k nearest neighbours (or to all points within radius \(\epsilon\)). Distances along this graph approximate distances along the manifold, and only local Euclidean distances are trusted — which is exactly the regime in which they are reliable.
MethodWhat it preservesMachinery

Reading t-SNE and UMAP plots responsibly
Both are visualisation tools, not distance-preserving embeddings. Cluster sizes in a t-SNE plot are meaningless (the algorithm equalises density), inter-cluster distances are largely meaningless, and apparent clusters can appear in pure noise at low perplexity. Never feed t-SNE output into a downstream model; use it to look, then verify what you saw by another means [13].

Notice what just happened. Isomap, LLE, Laplacian eigenmaps, t-SNE and UMAP all begin by throwing away the ambient coordinates and keeping only a graph. Once that step is taken, the natural next question is what one can do with data that arrives as a graph, with no coordinates to discard. That is Part II.

Summary

  • k-means minimises within-cluster inertia; the problem is NP-hard [3] and Lloyd's algorithm [1] finds only a local optimum.
  • k-means++ seeding gives an O(log k) approximation guarantee before any iteration [4].
  • Inertia cannot select k; use the silhouette [5] and domain knowledge.
  • k-means assumes spherical, balanced clusters — the failure that spectral methods fix.
  • PCA = eigenvectors of the covariance matrix = best low-rank approximation [9]; always centre, usually standardise.
  • Every non-linear embedding method starts by building a neighbourhood graph — the bridge into Part II.
