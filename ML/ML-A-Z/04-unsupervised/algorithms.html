Lloyd's algorithm for k-means

Alternating method \(\cdot\) each half is exactly solvable

Divide observations into \(k\) groups so that each is tight around its own centre.

Input and output

Given. A table of observations, a number of groups \(k\), and \(k\) starting centres.

Produce. A group label for every observation, and the final centres.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Assign. Give every observation to whichever centre is nearest. For fixed centres this is the best possible assignment — there is nothing to improve.
  2. Update. Move every centre to the average position of the observations assigned to it. For a fixed assignment this is the best possible placement, because the average is the point minimising total squared distance.
  3. Check. If no observation changed group, stop — nothing can change any more.
  4. Repeat. Otherwise go back and assign again.
  5. Restart and keep the best. The result depends entirely on where you started. Run the whole thing several times from different starts and keep the run with the smallest total squared distance.

Why the answer is correct

Both steps can only reduce the total squared distance, and there are finitely many ways to divide the observations into groups. A quantity that never rises over a finite set of possibilities must settle.

Why it stops

It settles at a local best, not necessarily the global one. Finding the global best is computationally intractable, even for two groups in the plane.

What it costs

\(n k d\) per round, and typically few rounds.

Where it goes wrong
  • The objective assumes round, similarly sized groups. On elongated, crescent-shaped or nested-ring data it fails predictably, because the boundary it can draw is always straight and halfway between two centres.
  • A group can lose all its members. Decide in advance what to do — usually re-seed that centre at the worst-fitted observation.
  • Inputs on different scales distort every distance. Standardise first.

Careful seeding

Initialisation \(\cdot\) comes with a guarantee

Choose starting centres that are spread out, so the alternating method begins somewhere sensible.

Input and output

Given. The observations and the number of groups \(k\).

Produce. \(k\) starting centres.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Take the first at random. Any observation will do.
  2. Measure everything's distance to the nearest chosen centre. For each observation, the distance to whichever centre so far is closest.
  3. Choose the next with probability proportional to that distance squared. Far-away observations are much likelier to be chosen, but nothing is impossible — which stops a single outlier from always being picked.
  4. Repeat until k centres are chosen. Recompute the distances after each choice, since a new centre may now be the nearest for many observations.
  5. Hand over. Run the alternating method from these centres.
Why the answer is correct

This seeding alone, before a single assign-update round, gives an expected total squared distance within a factor of order \(\log k\) of the best possible. Uniform random seeding offers no such bound at all.

Why it stops

\(k\) rounds.

What it costs

\(n k d\) overall — comparable to a single round of the alternating method, so it is essentially free.

Where it goes wrong
  • Choosing the farthest point deterministically rather than at random loses the guarantee and picks outliers every time.
Worth knowing

This is the default in every serious implementation, and it usually makes the subsequent rounds converge faster as well.

Principal component analysis

Direct method \(\cdot\) an eigenvalue problem

Find the directions along which the data vary most, and describe each observation by its position along a few of them.

Input and output

Given. A table of observations and a number of components to keep.

Produce. New coordinates, and the share of the total variation each component explains.

The procedure, step by step

  1. Centre every column. Subtract each column's average. This is not optional: without it the first component simply points at the overall average and tells you nothing.
  2. Standardise if the units differ. If one column is in metres and another in milligrams, rescale each to unit spread. Otherwise the component analysis is really an analysis of which column has the largest units.
  3. Form the covariance summary. For each pair of columns, average the product of their centred values.
  4. Find its principal directions. Compute the directions in which this summary is largest, together with how large it is along each. They come out mutually perpendicular and ordered.
  5. Keep the leading few. Choose how many by looking at the share of total variation explained, or by finding the elbow in that sequence.
  6. Project. Describe each observation by its position along the kept directions.

Two directions carry 98 % of the variation, so the four-column table can be described by two numbers per observation with very little loss.

Why the answer is correct

The directions found are simultaneously those of greatest spread and those giving the closest low-dimensional approximation to the original data. The two descriptions are equivalent.

Why it stops

Direct computation, no iteration.

What it costs

Cubic in the number of columns. Computed from the data table directly rather than from the covariance summary, for numerical accuracy.

Where it goes wrong
  • It can only rotate. Data lying on a curved surface will not be unrolled, and the number of components will be overstated.
  • Components are chosen for variance, not usefulness. A low-variance direction may be the one carrying the signal you want.
  • Signs are arbitrary: a component and its negative are equally valid.

The silhouette score

Evaluation \(\cdot\) has an actual optimum

Judge a grouping without knowing the truth, and use it to choose how many groups there are.

Input and output

Given. Observations with an assigned group label.

Produce. A number between −1 and 1 for each observation, and their average.

The procedure, step by step

  1. For one observation, measure how well it fits its own group. Average distance from it to the other members of its group, excluding itself. Small is good.
  2. Measure how well it would fit the best alternative. For every other group, the average distance from it to that group's members. Take the smallest.
  3. Combine. Subtract the first from the second and divide by whichever is larger. Near 1 means comfortably placed; near 0 means on a boundary; negative means it belongs to a different group.
  4. Average over all observations. The result summarises the whole grouping.
  5. Repeat for each candidate number of groups. Unlike total squared distance, which always falls as you add groups, this peaks at a sensible number.

Total squared distance keeps falling and so cannot choose. The silhouette peaks at three, which is the answer.

Why the answer is correct

It measures whether observations are closer to their own group than to any other — a property any reasonable grouping should have, and one that does not depend on which method produced it.

Why it stops

Direct.

What it costs

Quadratic in the number of observations, since every pair is compared. On large data, compute it on a random subsample.

Where it goes wrong
  • It rewards round, well-separated groups, so it will quietly favour methods that produce them. On genuinely elongated groups a low score does not necessarily mean the grouping is wrong.
  • No internal measure can tell you the grouping is meaningful. It can only tell you it is geometrically clean.
