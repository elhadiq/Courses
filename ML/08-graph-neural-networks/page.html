8.1 The message-passing framework

Section 7 closed with three defects of shallow embeddings: no parameter sharing, no inductive capability, and no use of node features. All three are cured by the same move — replace the lookup table with a learned function of the neighbourhood. Gilmer et al. [8] showed that essentially every architecture proposed for graphs fits one template, applied for L rounds:

\[m_{u}^{(l)} = \mathbf{\operatorname{AGGREGATE}}^{(l)}( \{ h_{v}^{(l-1)} : v \in N(u) \} )\]
\[h_{u}^{(l)} = \mathbf{\operatorname{UPDATE}}^{(l)}( h_{u}^{(l-1)}, m_{u}^{(l)} )\]

with \(h_{u}^{(0)} = x_{u}\), the input features. For graph-level tasks a final READOUT (sum, mean or max over all nodes) collapses the node representations into one vector.

The permutation requirement
AGGREGATE takes a multiset and must be invariant to the order in which neighbours are listed — there is no canonical ordering (Section 5.1). Sum, mean and max qualify; concatenation does not. The whole layer is then permutation-equivariant: relabelling the nodes relabels the outputs identically. This is the same design principle that makes CNNs translation-equivariant, and it is the organising idea of geometric deep learning [15].

Two immediate consequences, both from Section 5.2. After L rounds, \(h_{u}^{(L)}\) depends exactly on the L-hop neighbourhood of u — multiplying by A is one hop. And because real graphs are small-world, L = 2 or 3 already covers a large fraction of the graph, which is why GNNs are shallow where image networks are deep.

The idea is not new: Gori, Monfardini and Scarselli [1] [2] proposed recurrent message passing to a fixed point in 2005. What changed after 2016 was making the recursion a fixed, small number of differentiable layers trained end-to-end.

8.2 From spectral filters to the GCN

The modern line starts spectrally. Bruna et al. [3] defined convolution on graphs through the Laplacian eigenbasis of Section 5.3: \(g_{\theta}\) ⋆ x = \(Ug_{\theta}(\Lambda )U^{T}x\). Two problems: computing U costs O(\(n^3\)), and the learned filter has O(n) parameters and no locality.

Defferrard et al. [4] fixed both by restricting \(g_{\theta}\) to a degree-K Chebyshev polynomial of the scaled Laplacian. A polynomial of degree K in L is automatically K-hop localised and needs only sparse matrix–vector products, giving O(\(K\cdot m\)) cost with K free parameters.

Kipf and Welling [5] then took the aggressive step: set K = 1, approximate \(\lambda_{\max} \approx 2\), and tie the two remaining coefficients (\(\theta_0 = -\theta_1\)). One layer collapses to

\[g_{\theta} ⋆ x \approx \theta ( I + D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2} ) x\]
The renormalisation trick
I + \(D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}\) has spectrum in [0, 2], so stacking L such layers multiplies the signal by up to \(2^{L}\) — numerically explosive. Kipf and Welling therefore replace it by \(\tilde{A} = A + I\) with degree matrix \(\tilde{D}\), giving \(\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\), whose spectrum lies in (−1, 1]. Adding self-loops is thus not cosmetic: it is what makes deep stacking numerically possible, and it also lets a node keep its own features.
\[H^{(l+1)} = \sigma ( \tilde{D}^{-1/2} \tilde{A} \tilde{D}^{-1/2} H^{(l)} W^{(l)} )\]

Per node this reads \(h_{u} \leftarrow \sigma ( W \sum_{v\in N(u)\cup \{u\}} h_{v} / \sqrt{\tilde{d}_{u}\tilde{d}_{v}} )\) — a symmetrically normalised weighted average of the neighbours, followed by a linear map and a non-linearity. The whole layer costs O(\(m\cdot d\)) and is a handful of lines of code.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class GCNLayer(nn.Module):
    """H' = sigma( D^-1/2 (A+I) D^-1/2 H W ). A_hat is precomputed and sparse."""
    def __init__(self, d_in, d_out):
        super().__init__()
        self.lin = nn.Linear(d_in, d_out, bias=False)

    def forward(self, H, A_hat_norm):
        return torch.sparse.mm(A_hat_norm, self.lin(H))


def normalize_adj(A, n):
    """D~^-1/2 (A + I) D~^-1/2 as a dense tensor (use sparse ops for real graphs)."""
    A_tilde = A + torch.eye(n)
    deg = A_tilde.sum(dim=1)
    d_inv_sqrt = deg.pow(-0.5)
    return d_inv_sqrt.unsqueeze(1) * A_tilde * d_inv_sqrt.unsqueeze(0)

Trained with a cross-entropy loss on the few labelled nodes, a two-layer GCN beat every prior method on the standard citation benchmarks by a wide margin. Note what it is doing: smoothing the feature signal over the graph, exactly the low-pass filter of Section 5.3, with a learned channel mixing. It works because those graphs are homophilous.

8.3 Aggregation variants

GraphSAGE — inductive, and sampled

The GCN as published is transductive: the normalised adjacency of the whole graph appears in the forward pass. Hamilton, Ying and Leskovec [6] made three changes that together yield an inductive model:

  1. Sample a fixed number of neighbours per node per layer (say 25 then 10), bounding the computation per node regardless of degree — essential on graphs with hubs (Section 5.4).
  2. Separate the node's own representation from the aggregate: \(h_{u} \leftarrow \sigma ( W \cdot\) CONCAT(\(h_{u}\), AGG{\(h_{v}\})\)), instead of folding self into the average.
  3. Generalise AGG to mean, max-pool (an MLP then element-wise max) or an LSTM over a random permutation.

Because the parameters are shared functions of features rather than per-node vectors, an unseen node — or an entirely unseen graph — can be embedded at inference time. This is what makes GNNs deployable on evolving production graphs.

Graph Attention Networks

Both GCN and GraphSAGE weight neighbours by a fixed function of degree. Veličković et al. [7] let the model learn the weights:

\[e_{uv} = LeakyReLU( a^{T}[Wh_{u} \| Wh_{v}] ), \alpha_{uv} = softmax_{v\in N(u)}(e_{uv})\]
\[h_{u}' = \sigma ( \sum_{v\in N(u)} \alpha_{uv} W h_{v} )\]

Attention is computed only over edges, so cost stays O(m); multiple heads are concatenated for stability, as in the Transformer. The gain is real on heterophilous or noisy graphs, where a node needs to ignore some neighbours — something a degree-based average cannot express. On clean homophilous benchmarks the advantage over GCN is modest.

8.4 How expressive is message passing?

A sharp theoretical answer exists, and it is one of the most useful results in the field.

The 1-Weisfeiler–Leman colour-refinement test [10]
Give every node the same initial colour. Repeat: recolour each node by hashing the pair (its own colour, the multiset of its neighbours' colours). Stop when the partition stabilises. Two graphs whose final colour multisets differ are certainly non-isomorphic; if the multisets agree, the test is inconclusive.
Theorem — Xu et al. (2019) [9]
Any message-passing GNN of the AGGREGATE/UPDATE form is at most as powerful as 1-WL at distinguishing non-isomorphic graphs. It attains that bound if and only if AGGREGATE, UPDATE and READOUT are all injective on multisets.

Mean and max aggregation are not injective: mean cannot tell {a, a} from {a}, and max cannot tell {a, b} from {a, a, b}. Sum can. This is the whole argument for GIN:

\[h_{u}^{(l)} = \operatorname{MLP}^{(l)}( (1 + \epsilon^{(l)}) h_{u}^{(l-1)} + \sum_{v\in N(u)} h_{v}^{(l-1)} )\]

Sum aggregation preserves multiset cardinality, and the MLP can approximate any injective function on it (universal approximation). GIN is provably 1-WL-expressive, and it is the strongest simple choice for graph-level tasks.

The bound bites. No message-passing GNN can count triangles, distinguish a 6-cycle from two disjoint triangles, or in general recognise cycle length — properties that matter enormously in chemistry, where rings determine molecular behaviour. Escapes exist: augment node features with random identifiers or structural descriptors (cycle counts, distances), use higher-order k-WL architectures, or add positional encodings from the Laplacian eigenvectors of Section 6 — the spectral tools return as a fix for the expressiveness gap.

8.5 The two pathologies of depth

Over-smoothing

Li, Han and Wu [11] identified the first, and it is exactly the operator theory of Section 5. The GCN propagation matrix is a low-pass filter; applying it L times drives every node representation toward the dominant eigenvector, which is (degree-weighted) constant. Node representations converge, and all discriminative information is destroyed.

\[as L \to \infty , h_{u}^{(L)} \to c \cdot \sqrt{\tilde{d}_{u}} \text{for every } u — \text{the Dirichlet energy } \sum_{(u,v)\in E}\| h_{u} - h_{v}\|^2 \text{decays geometrically }\]

Standard remedies: residual/skip connections, initial-residual plus identity mapping as in GCNII [14] (which trains usefully at 64 layers), PairNorm and similar normalisations, DropEdge, or simply not going deep — which, given small-world diameters, is usually the right call.

Interactive figure

The over-smoothing curve: normalised Dirichlet energy against depth.

Figure. Dirichlet energy after L rounds of GCN propagation, on a logarithmic vertical axis. The straight tail is the signature of geometric decay: the energy falls like \(\mu_2^{2L}\), where \(\mu_2\) is the second eigenvalue of \(\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\). The measured rate is printed on the figure. Note the honest detail: this particular graph has a strong bottleneck, hence a small spectral gap, so it takes tens of layers to collapse — whereas on a well-connected benchmark graph (\(\mu_2\) far from 1) the same curve reaches the floor by \(L \approx 5\). The rate, not the phenomenon, is graph-dependent.

Over-squashing

Alon and Yahav [12] identified the opposite failure. To use information from L hops away, a node must compress an exponentially growing receptive field (up to \(d^{L}\) nodes) into a fixed-size vector. Signal from distant nodes is squashed to nothing, so tasks with genuinely long-range dependencies fail even when the network is deep enough in principle.

Topping et al. [13] localised the cause: over-squashing concentrates at edges of high negative curvature — graph bottlenecks. Their remedy, graph rewiring, surgically adds edges around those bottlenecks. Other approaches add a virtual node connected to everything, or replace message passing with a graph transformer using global attention plus Laplacian positional encodings.

The two pathologies pull in opposite directions
Over-smoothing punishes depth; over-squashing punishes the shallowness that avoids it. There is no setting of L that escapes both on a graph with long-range dependencies and a bottleneck. Recognising which one you face — measure the Dirichlet energy for the first, the task's dependency range for the second — is more useful than trying architectures at random.

8.6 Practical guidance

  • Establish baselines first. Logistic regression on node features alone, and label propagation (Section 5.3) on structure alone. Shchur et al. [16] showed that with fair hyperparameter budgets and multiple random splits, the ranking of published GNNs on the standard benchmarks largely dissolves — and simple baselines are often competitive.
  • Measure homophily before choosing. If h is low, a GCN will underperform an MLP; reach for GAT, or for architectures designed for heterophily.
  • Use 2–3 layers unless you have a specific reason and a specific remedy for over-smoothing.
  • Report mean and standard deviation over several splits and seeds. GNN variance across seeds routinely exceeds the gaps between competing methods [16].
  • Sample neighbours (GraphSAGE-style) or use cluster-based batching as soon as the graph does not fit in memory.

Summary

  • Every GNN is AGGREGATE / UPDATE message passing [8]; L rounds see the L-hop neighbourhood.
  • The GCN [5] is a first-order polynomial filter, \(\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\)HW — the renormalisation trick keeps the spectrum bounded.
  • GraphSAGE [6] adds inductive capability and neighbour sampling; GAT [7] learns the neighbour weights.
  • Message passing is bounded by 1-WL [9] [10]; sum aggregation (GIN) attains the bound, mean and max do not.
  • Over-smoothing [11] and over-squashing [12] are opposing limits on depth.
  • Benchmark honestly: strong non-GNN baselines, multiple splits, reported variance [16].
