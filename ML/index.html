How the course is organised

One argument, not a list of algorithms

Part I develops classical machine learning and, at each step, exposes the assumption that will later fail: least squares assumes a linear relationship, k-means assumes spherical clusters, PCA assumes a linear manifold. Part II shows that relaxing those assumptions leads, repeatedly and from several directions, to the same object — a graph and its Laplacian — and then builds modern graph learning on top of it.

The thread running through both parts

Section 4 ends by observing that every non-linear embedding method begins by discarding coordinates and keeping only a neighbourhood graph. Section 5 formalises the graph and its Laplacian. Section 6 shows that clustering by graph cuts is an eigenproblem on that Laplacian, and that its embedding is the Laplacian eigenmap. Section 7 shows that walk-based node embeddings implicitly factorise a matrix of walk probabilities, so they too are spectral methods. Section 8 derives the graph convolution as a first-order polynomial filter in that same Laplacian, and explains over-smoothing as the spectral gap at work. One matrix, five sections.

Prerequisites

What you need before starting

  • Linear algebra — matrix multiplication, eigenvalues and eigenvectors of symmetric matrices, the spectral theorem. Sections 6 and 8 lean on this heavily.
  • Multivariable calculus — gradients, and what it means for a function to be convex.
  • Probability — expectation, variance, conditional distributions, and the idea of an independent sample.
  • Python — comfortable with lists, dictionaries and functions. The practice questions use nothing beyond the standard library.

On the citations

Roughly 110 primary sources

Every substantive claim is attributed. Definitions, theorems and empirical findings carry a numbered citation to the paper or book that established them, and each section ends with a full reference list including a DOI or permanent link. The bibliography runs from Kirchhoff (1847) and Legendre (1805) to the geometric deep learning and over-squashing literature of the 2020s. Where a result is commonly misattributed, the course points at the original.

What the course does not claim

Several figures deliberately show a method failing: k-means on non-spherical clusters, the eigengap when no community structure remains, two graphs that colour refinement cannot separate, and a network whose over-smoothing is slow because the graph has a bottleneck. Knowing the boundary of a method matters at least as much as knowing the method, and these boundaries are where most applied mistakes live.
