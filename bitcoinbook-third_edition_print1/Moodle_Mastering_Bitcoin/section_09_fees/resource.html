Transaction Fees

Fees are how you bid for scarce block space. This section explains fee rate versus absolute fee, how wallets estimate what to pay, and how to speed up a stuck transaction.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why fees exist and who ultimately pays them.
  • Distinguish the absolute fee (satoshis) from the fee rate (sat/vB).
  • Compute a fee rate, and the fee needed to hit a target rate.
  • Describe RBF and CPFP for un-sticking a low-fee transaction.

1. Why fees exist

Each block can hold only a limited amount of transaction data (about 4 million weight units, Section 6). Far more transactions want in than will fit, so there is a market for space. A transaction offers a fee — recall from Section 2 that the fee is simply inputs minus outputs — and miners, wanting to earn the most, tend to pick the transactions offering the most value per unit of space. Pay more, get in sooner.

As the block subsidy shrinks with each halving (Section 1), fees are gradually becoming the main reward that pays miners to keep securing the network.

2. Absolute fee vs. fee rate

This is the distinction that matters most. Two different measurements:

  • The absolute fee is the total number of satoshis paid: inputs minus outputs. It is what the miner pockets.
  • The fee rate is the fee per unit of size, measured in satoshis per virtual byte (sat/vB), using the virtual size from Section 6.

Miners care about the rate, not the total, because space is what they are selling. A 1,000 sat fee on a tiny 100 vB transaction (10 sat/vB) is more attractive than a 1,000 sat fee on a bloated 1,000 vB transaction (1 sat/vB), even though the absolute fee is identical.

The two formulas

fee_rate (sat/vB) = absolute_fee ÷ vsize

absolute_fee (sat) = fee_rate × vsize

Worked example. A transaction of 141 vB paying 1,410 sat has a fee rate of 1410 ÷ 141 = 10 sat/vB. Conversely, if the mempool is busy and you want 20 sat/vB on that same 141 vB transaction, you must pay 20 × 141 = 2,820 sat.

3. Estimating the right fee rate

Pay too little and your transaction waits (maybe for days); pay too much and you waste money. Wallets therefore estimate a fee rate from recent network activity — how full recent blocks were and what rates got confirmed. Bitcoin Core exposes this through estimatesmartfee. Typical guidance: a higher rate for “confirm within 1 block,” a lower rate for “confirm within a day.”

UrgencyTypical rate (illustrative)
Next blockhigh sat/vB
Within ~an hourmedium sat/vB
Within a daylow sat/vB

4. Un-sticking a transaction: RBF and CPFP

Suppose you paid too little and your transaction is stuck. Two mechanisms let you rescue it:

Replace-By-Fee (RBF). You re-broadcast the same transaction with a higher fee, replacing the stuck one. For the network to accept the replacement, it must genuinely be better. The core rules are simple: the replacement must pay a higher absolute fee and a higher fee rate than the original.

replacement valid ⇔ new_fee > old_fee  AND  new_rate > old_rate

Child-Pays-For-Parent (CPFP). If you received a low-fee transaction and want it confirmed, you cannot change it — but you can spend its output in a new child transaction that pays a high fee. A miner who wants the child's fee must include the parent too (since the child depends on it), so both confirm together. The child “pays for” the parent.

Rule of thumb. Use RBF when you are the sender of the stuck transaction; use CPFP when you are the receiver.
